Chapter One

The Ridge With No Name

The ridge had no name on any map worth trusting.

It ran northeast along the spine of the Blue Ridge like a scar that the mountains had simply decided to keep, a narrow backbone of ancient granite stubbled with wind-tortured spruce and frost-killed fern. At six thousand feet the sky pressed down harder than it did anywhere else GoldenPaw had ever stood, and he had stood in a great many high and impossible places. He crouched now at the very lip of a granite outcrop, one broad paw braced against cool stone, the other hanging loose over nothing but three hundred feet of open air and the dark, breathing forest below. The last of the October sun caught the rosette-speckled fur along his shoulders and turned it to hammered copper, to raw amber, to something that almost deserved the name he carried.

His ears swiveled without any instruction from his conscious mind — left, southeast, tracking a sound that had not yet become a sound. A vibration, more accurately. The mountains were old enough to feel things before they arrived.

He had learned to trust that.

The Appalachians breathed differently than other ranges. The Rockies were cathedral, all vertical ambition and pale limestone altars. These mountains were something older, something that had already made its peace with erosion and storm and the slow grind of geological time. They were rounded at the top and deeply creased between, their slopes quilted in a canopy so dense and so varied that from above they looked like the pelt of some enormous sleeping animal — oak and hickory bleeding into beech, beech giving way to the dark congregation of red spruce, all of it burning now in the last fury of autumn, yellow and orange and a red so violent it seemed personal. GoldenPaw loved them for exactly that quality. The humility of a mountain that had survived long enough to stop showing off.

He had been patrolling the corridor since before dawn.

Not that anyone had asked him to. That was the arrangement he'd made with himself three years ago when he'd first come to this range — patrol it like it mattered, like the people tucked into its hollows and perched on its shelved ridgelines were worth the sleeplessness and the cold and the particular loneliness that came with flying too fast for ordinary company. The arrangement worked. It worked because he never examined it too closely.

The vibration resolved itself.

Rotors. Heavy-lift, twin-engine, running without navigation lights in a federal wilderness corridor where that kind of flight was not legal and was not accidental.

GoldenPaw rose to his full height. The wind off the ridge pushed back against his chest and he let it, reading it — northeast, fourteen miles per hour, carrying woodsmoke from a valley he knew, carrying the early iron smell of weather building somewhere over Tennessee. His tail moved in a slow counter-balance arc behind him, the black rings at its tip disappearing and reappearing in the failing light. His eyes, gold as the name suggested and capable of resolving detail in conditions that would blind a human instrument, found the shape before the sound had finished announcing itself.

Black helicopter. Military-surplus configuration, retrofitted. Flying the valley below his ridge at a speed that spoke of purpose rather than search. Cargo hook occupied.

And suspended from that hook, swinging with an eerie passivity in the rotor wash, a steel container roughly the size of a freight elevator car, painted the unremarkable gray of something that did not want to be remembered.

He did not know what was in it.

He was going to find out.

GoldenPaw rolled his shoulders, felt the familiar current stir beneath his skin — that deep electrical warmth that lived somewhere between muscle and instinct and whatever it was the scientists had written about him in their reports, the ones he'd never officially been shown. He stepped off the granite lip into the open air, and the Appalachian dark rose up around him like a hand catching something it had been waiting a long time to hold.

Chapter Two

Raven Fork

He fell the first hundred feet on purpose.

The cheetah in him demanded it — that pure, unthinking surrender to gravity before the current answered, before the warmth beneath his skin became something structural rather than merely felt. It was a discipline he had arrived at slowly and somewhat painfully in the early months, when the power was still a negotiation rather than a language. Now the falling was almost ceremonial. A reminder to whatever part of him remained ordinary animal that the extraordinary part was not a replacement for it but a continuation.

At the hundred-and-first foot, the current answered.

He leveled without slowing appreciably, threading the gap between two outcrops of exposed granite at a speed that turned the rock faces into smears of gray and shadow on either side. The cold hit him differently in flight — sharper, more particular, a thousand individual points of sensation rather than the diffuse background chill of simply standing at altitude. His whiskers registered every small turbulence. His tail, longer and more expressive than any cheetah's had a right to be, made constant micro-corrections that he was not consciously aware of making.

Below and northeast, the helicopter was threading its own path through the mountains' dark geometry.

The pilot knew these valleys. That was the first thing GoldenPaw noted as he dropped lower, matching altitude with the treeline, letting the canopy's broken surface swallow his silhouette. An unfamiliar pilot, a nervous pilot, would have taken the high route — up and over the ridgelines, burning fuel for the comfort of open sky. This one was flying the drainages, following the cold air pooling in the creek beds, the invisible highways that wind carves over centuries between shoulder and hollow and gap. That was local knowledge, or something bought from someone who had it.

The second thing he noted: the container was not swinging anymore.

When he had first spotted it from the ridge, it had moved with the lax indifference of dead weight, pendulumming gently as the helicopter banked. Now it hung in a precise, unnatural stillness. Internal stabilization. Whatever was inside was worth the engineering cost of making sure it arrived undisturbed.

GoldenPaw pulled his arms in and accelerated.

The forest thirty feet below him was a fast, dense darkness. He read it by its sounds as much as its shapes — the wind's register dropping as the canopy thickened ahead, the sound of a creek swelling somewhere to his left, the brief, startled percussion of something large moving through brush at the noise of his passage. A bear, probably. October, before the long sleep, still restless with the last of autumn's business. He understood that restlessness from the inside.

He closed within a quarter mile of the helicopter before he eased off, matching its airspeed and holding position in its six o'clock where the rotor wash and engine noise would mask whatever small disturbance his own movement created. Military surplus, he had noted from the ridge. At this distance he could be more specific. The airframe was a Sikorsky variant, the kind that had moved through a dozen different owners since its decommission — government auction to private contractor, private contractor to whatever came next on the ledger. The modifications were not cheap. The running-lights override, the cargo hook reinforcement, the matte black repaint laid over something that had once been olive drab. Somebody was spending money to appear as though they did not want to spend money.

The container, from here, showed him its seam.

A single, precise seam running the full circumference — not a structural weld but a designed separation point, a lid, a hinge system he could not yet see the logic of. There were no markings on the gray surface. Not even the absence of markings. It was the sort of blankness that was itself a kind of information, if you knew how to read negative space.

He moved up along the helicopter's port side, forty feet out, keeping below its crew's sightlines.

The mountains were closing in. The valley was narrowing, the ridge walls rising on either side, and GoldenPaw recognized the geography with a prickle of attention — he was three, perhaps four miles northwest of a place the locals called Raven Fork, a confluence of two small creeks that had no particular importance on any map he'd been shown by any agency, but which he had noted six weeks earlier during a routine patrol because someone had recently built something there. Not a hunting camp. The footprint was too deliberate, the clearing too precise. He had filed it in the portion of his mind reserved for things that had not yet declared themselves.

It was declaring itself now.

The helicopter began to descend.

GoldenPaw bled off speed and dropped lower, using a long hogback ridge to his left as cover, letting himself fall behind the aircraft's sight envelope as it slowed and tilted into its approach. He found a perch — a tall dead hemlock, silver-white in the dimness, standing at the ridge's edge above the Raven Fork clearing — and took it, gripping a thick upper branch with both hands, his claws finding the wood without thought.

Below him, floodlights came on.

Not bright — deliberately not bright, the kind of illumination calibrated to be useful without being visible from any ridge a mile out. But to his eyes it was sufficient, more than sufficient, and what it showed him made the current beneath his skin do something it had never quite done before.

Not surge. Not spike.

Go very, very quiet.

The clearing was larger than he remembered, and it was not empty. Four vehicles were staged along its western edge — heavy-framed, identical, the kind of vehicles that were themselves a kind of argument about what their owners were willing to do. A dozen figures moved with the crisp, choreographed efficiency of people who had rehearsed this specific sequence enough times to remove hesitation from it. They wore the practical dark clothing of people who thought about not being seen as a professional consideration rather than an outfit choice. And at the center of the clearing, already waiting, already open, already lit from within by that same careful non-brightness, was a second container.

Larger than the one on the helicopter's hook.

And it was not gray.

It was white, very clean, entirely out of place against the October mud and forest dark and the general vocabulary of concealment that everything else in that clearing was speaking. It sat in the clearing the way a word sits in a sentence that was not built to hold it — wrong in a way that reorganized the meaning of everything around it.

The helicopter settled toward its landing zone. The cables went taut as the crew began lowering the gray container toward the open white one.

GoldenPaw watched, and did not move, and made himself be the mountains for a moment — old, and patient, and full of the particular attention of something that had survived long enough to understand that the worst mistakes were the ones made before you knew what you were looking at.

The container reached the white receptacle.

And something inside it — something he had not heard, something that had not been audible over the rotors until the rotors throttled back — went silent.

It had been making a sound, very low, very constant, the kind of frequency that his body had been registering as unease rather than as input. He understood that now only because the sound had stopped, and the sudden absence of it hit him like a step onto ground that was not where the ground was supposed to be.

Whatever was in that gray container had been alive.

Whatever was in it might still be.

His grip on the dead hemlock tightened until the silver wood gave a soft complaint under his claws, and below him the figures in the clearing moved with their rehearsed efficiency around the joined containers, and the Appalachian dark pressed in from all sides with the attentive, unreadable patience of mountains that had seen worse than this and were prepared, without particular feeling, to see it again.

The name came back to him twenty minutes later, and it came back the way names always did when they had been filed under not yet — suddenly and completely, as if it had been waiting with its coat on.

Harlan Voss.

He had heard the name twice in three years, which was twice more than coincidence allowed. The first time from a forest service ranger named Delia Cooke who had mentioned it in passing during a conversation about illegal ginseng poaching that had, in retrospect, not actually been about ginseng. The second time from a signals intercept that an anonymous contact had forwarded to him through a method that required no names from either party, attached to a grid coordinate in Madison County that he had flown out to investigate and found scrubbed clean three hours before he arrived.

Harlan Voss. Not a name that appeared in any database he had access to through legitimate channels. A name that existed, therefore, in the kind of record that did not want to be a record.

The figures below him had finished their work. The gray container was secured inside the white one, the lid sealed with a sound like a verdict, and half the vehicles were already moving, their lights still dark, feeling their way down a logging road that had been here long enough to appear on pre-war maps and short enough for its recent improvements to go unremarked.

Two vehicles remained. Four figures.

They were waiting for something.

GoldenPaw eased himself from the hemlock branch by degrees, moving to the ridge's face and descending along it in a careful, ground-hugging traverse that kept rock between him and the clearing's remaining floodlights. The October leaves were dry — that was the problem with autumn in these mountains, the beautiful colors came with the liability of everything underfoot turning acoustic. He moved on the balls of his feet, placing each step with the precision that had been bred into his kind over millennia, and the leaves barely whispered.

The current under his skin was still quiet.

He had learned to pay attention to that.

The waiting figures' attention was oriented north. He went south, circling the clearing at the tree line, using the hemlock shadows and the steady background sound of Raven Fork's water over stone to cover his movement. At the clearing's southern edge he stopped, crouched behind a granite shelf that had heaved up out of the hillside at some point in the mountains' ancient and unobserved career, and he waited with them, watching what they were watching.

Three minutes.

Then headlights appeared on the northern track.

A single vehicle, nothing like the others — civilian-registered, nondescript, the kind of vehicle that moved through small mountain towns without generating a second look or a first conversation. It stopped at the clearing's edge and one person got out, and that person walked to the white container and stood before it in the way that only owners stand before things.

GoldenPaw could not see the face clearly at this distance, even with his eyes.

But he could see the posture. The particular quality of stillness in it. The way the figure laid one hand flat against the white surface, not checking it, not inspecting it — just touching it, the way you touch something that is finally yours after a long patience.

The current stirred.

Not a surge. Not yet. But something moving through the deep electrical warmth of it, a preliminary tremor, a question the power was beginning to formulate about whether this was the kind of situation that required an answer soon.

GoldenPaw pressed his back against the cold granite and looked up through the bare branches at the October stars and thought, with the methodical clarity that worked better than urgency in the moments before urgency became unavoidable, about what he knew and what he didn't know and the precise width of the gap between the two.

What he knew: an unregistered aircraft had transported an unregistered cargo into a federal wilderness corridor and transferred that cargo to persons unknown at a location that had been prepared in advance for exactly this purpose. Something in that cargo had been alive, or had been producing the biological-adjacent frequencies of something alive, until approximately four minutes ago. The operation had the operational signature of significant resourcing — not improvisational, not amateur, not the kind of thing that happened once.

What he didn't know: everything else.

The figure at the white container withdrew their hand and turned back toward the waiting vehicle, and GoldenPaw watched the profile against the fading floodlights and noted the height, the gait, the particular tilt of the head that people carry without knowing they carry it.

Not Harlan Voss. Or not the Harlan Voss that the fragments of description he'd assembled over three years suggested.

Someone who reported to Harlan Voss, then. Or someone who had paid Harlan Voss. Or someone who did not know Harlan Voss at all, which would mean his two data points were not a line but merely two unrelated dots, and he was three years of night patrols from understanding what was actually happening in the spine of these old mountains.

The vehicles left. The floodlights went dark. The clearing returned to the October darkness with a completeness that suggested the lights had been engineered to leave no trace of their warmth in the surrounding trees.

GoldenPaw waited another four minutes before he moved, because four minutes was enough time for the kind of person who planned this carefully to have left something behind.

Nothing moved in the clearing.

He came out of the tree line at a walk and crossed to where the white container had stood. The ground told him what the dark wanted to conceal — compressed earth in a rectangle too precise for anything natural, four parallel grooves from the container's base ribs, the faint chemical trace on the air of something sealed under pressure that had, briefly, not been sealed. He crouched and pressed his palm flat to the ground, the way the figure had pressed their hand to the container, and he held very still and listened to the shape of what had happened here.

The Appalachians breathed.

Somewhere down the valley, a barred owl issued its question to the dark and the dark gave it back without answering.

GoldenPaw straightened, turned northeast along the track the main convoy had taken, and considered what came next with the focused, unrushed attention of a predator that had never once in its career mistaken speed for readiness.

He was going to need to know what was in that container.

He was going to need to know it before whoever had taken it needed it to do whatever it had been taken to do.

And somewhere in the crease between those two requirements was the shape of every mistake he could possibly make, laid out in the dark ahead of him like a country he had not yet mapped.

He started northeast at a ground-eating lope, the current warm and ready beneath his skin, the mountains steady and immense on either side, and the truck lights were long gone but their direction was not, and the night was long, and GoldenPaw had never once in three years failed to use the whole of it.
