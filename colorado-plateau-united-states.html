Chapter One

Readings Off

The sun bled copper and rust across the Colorado Plateau as it sank behind the sandstone towers of Canyonlands, painting the sky in shades that looked borrowed from the rock itself — burnt sienna, deep ochre, the trembling violet of a bruise healing at the edges. It was the kind of sunset that made tourists stop their rental cars along Route 191 and reach for their phones, desperate to capture something that cameras never quite managed to hold.

Dax Aurelio didn't stop for sunsets anymore. He was already moving through them.

He ran along the ridge of a narrow sandstone fin above the confluence of the Green and Colorado Rivers, his spotted paws barely kissing the stone before springing forward again, each stride covering ground in the effortless, rolling lope that was simply the way he existed in the world — the way a cheetah had always existed, long before the world had given him anything more complicated to do with it. The warm air parted around his face. His tawny ears flattened. The black tear-tracks that ran from the inner corners of his golden eyes down to the edges of his mouth caught the last light, and for a moment he was just an animal and the plateau and the failing sun.

Then the satellite phone clipped to his vest buzzed twice.

He slowed, the pads of all four feet spreading wide against the cooling rock, and glanced at the screen. A single line of text from Ranger Nadia Swiftwind, his closest contact at the Canyonlands field station.

Sulfur smell near Upheaval Dome. Readings off. You should look.

Dax exhaled through his nose. Upheaval Dome was thirteen miles north, a bizarre circular crater of tortured rock that geologists still argued about — impact crater, ancient salt collapse, the plateau's own private argument with itself over what it was supposed to be. He had been out here tonight for something simpler: exercise, solitude, the particular silence that only the canyon country could offer. A silence so complete you could hear a raven's wings from half a mile away, could hear the river whispering two thousand feet below.

He looked at the message again.

Readings off.

Nadia wasn't someone who alarmed easily. She'd worked the backcountry for eleven years, had walked into a wildfire camp at night carrying water, had once talked a stranded hiker down from a panic attack over a satellite connection with barely enough signal to carry a single voice. "Readings off" from Nadia meant something.

He typed back: How off?

Her reply came in seconds. Atmospheric sensors showing volatile organic compounds. Something's venting. No natural gas infrastructure anywhere near there. I checked twice.

On my way, he sent. Then he tucked the phone away, rolled his spotted shoulders once beneath the fitted carbon-fiber vest that served as the closest thing he kept to a uniform, and ran.

* * *

The name GoldenPaw had not been his idea.

It had started, as so many things did, online — specifically in the comment section of a shaky cell phone video uploaded by a hiker named Brendan Cho, who had been descending the Syncline Loop Trail eighteen months ago when he'd watched a spotted figure moving along the canyon rim at a speed that his footage could only hint at: a blur of gold and black against the red walls, there and then gone, leaving only a small glowing after-impression on the stone where the figure's front paw had pushed off into the next stride. The comment that received the most upvotes within the first hour read simply: ok but did anyone else see the golden paw prints he left?? glowing??? what is happening in Utah????

The prints had puzzled even Dax, at first.

He'd first noticed them a year before Brendan Cho's video, about six weeks after whatever had happened in the mine shaft below the Manti-La Sal National Forest — the incident that he didn't talk about, that he had no clean explanation for, that had left him alive when he should not have been. The glow lasted only seconds, fading the way an ember fades: from bright to orange to dark. It didn't burn the rock. It didn't leave a lasting mark. But the prints were undeniably there, undeniably luminous, and on two occasions, standing in the dark canyon interior, he'd looked down at his own forepaws and watched the faint internal light pulse under the skin the way a heartbeat would if hearts could shine.

He'd grown used to them, the way you grew used to any strange fact of your own body.

The speed had always been his — that was pure cheetah, pure biology, the long lever-arm of his spine and the deep-keeled chest and the semi-retractable claws that bit into ground like cleats. But since the mine, the speed was something more. Since the mine, when he pushed into a real sprint, the canyon walls blurred in a way that had nothing to do with natural muscle and bone. He had clocked himself once, on a long straight stretch of hardpan north of Dead Horse Point, using two fixed cameras and a stopwatch app on Nadia's old tablet. He had not shown her the result, had just handed the tablet back and said thank you and gone to sit at the edge of the mesa and watch the river for a long time.

He didn't know what he was. He knew what he did. For now, that was enough of a definition.

* * *

The crater at Upheaval Dome was older than imagination — roughly five miles across, the exposed rock at its center so twisted and upthrust that it looked like something enormous had tried to push its way up from below and then thought better of it. Geologists believed it was half a billion years old if it was anything, which meant that whatever Dax was running toward had chosen as its location a place that had been strange long before humans had walked the plateau, long before cheetahs had been brought across the ocean to live in the American Southwest, long before any of the complicated and improbable machinery of the present had ground into motion.

He smelled it before he saw anything.

Not sulfur, exactly — sulfur was the crude approximation, the word humans reached for when the real compound was something more industrial, something with sharper edges than rotten eggs. This was sulfur dioxide mixed with something else, something that hit the back of his nasal cavity like a cold wire, chemical and clean and wrong. His ears swept forward. He slowed from his long sprint to a careful trot, paws quiet on the sandstone, body low.

The crater was pale in the risen moonlight, the upthrust white rock at its center glowing faintly silver against the darkness of the surrounding depression. Nothing moved. The desert at this hour was typically a living thing — nighthawks, owls, the shuffle of a kangaroo rat, the distant cough of a coyote somewhere in the canyon. Tonight there was none of that. The animals had already read something in the air that Dax was only now reading with his more sophisticated and more oblivious senses.

He moved to the crater rim and crouched, looking down.

Three figures in the crater floor. One of them wore a hard hat with a mounted light, its beam cutting a white line through the darkness. The other two were loading something from a flatbed vehicle — a vehicle that had no business being down there, had certainly not come down any marked road, which meant it had come cross-country, had navigated the slickrock in the dark with a confidence that required either detailed prior knowledge or mapping technology significantly better than what you could buy off the shelf. Long metal cylinders, each about four feet long. The kind of container that carried something pressurized.

Dax counted them. Twelve cylinders already loaded onto the vehicle's bed. Two more being carried now.

The smell was coming from an open shaft near the crater's upthrust center, a man-made hole framed with steel casing that had been anchored into the rock with obvious care and no obvious permit. Someone had drilled into the oldest rock on the plateau and was pumping something out of it — or had been pumping something in.

He studied the three figures. They moved with the efficiency of professionals, no wasted gestures, no conversation. Hard hats, dark clothing, the kind of practical anonymity that was not accidental. The vehicle had no visible plates on the rear; it was angled so that even if he moved around the crater, he'd be coming toward them before he could read whatever was on the front.

Dax had no badge. He had no authority recognized by any government body. What he had were quick feet and enough good sense to know that twelve pressurized cylinders of something that smelled like this, pulled from an illegal bore-hole in a national park, was not a conversation to have by himself at ten-thirty at night in the middle of the desert.

He eased back from the rim, put his back against the rock, and pulled out the satellite phone.

Nadia answered on the second ring.

"You're there," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Three people, a vehicle, fourteen cylinders that I can count, and a bore-shaft someone cased into the center rock. Whatever they pulled out of it smells like SO2 and something else I can't name." He kept his voice level, professional, the tone he used when he needed her to stay equally calm. "I need you to call the park service emergency line and then I need you to call EPA Region Eight. Tell them both the same thing: illegal extraction activity, Upheaval Dome, possible hazardous materials."

"Already on it," she said, and he could hear her moving, chair legs against the floor of the station. "How long before they move?"

He looked back over the rim. The two figures had loaded the last cylinders. The one with the headlamp was pulling up the casing bolts, disassembling the wellhead with quick practiced motions. "They're already cleaning up. Fifteen minutes before they're mobile."

A pause. "That's not enough time, Dax."

"I know."

"So what are you going to do?"

He watched the headlamp swing down toward the vehicle. He watched the cylinders strapped under canvas on the flatbed. Fourteen cylinders of something extracted from rock half a billion years old, heading god knows where for god knows what purpose, by three people who had gone to considerable trouble and expense to be here tonight and to leave no trace.

"I'm going to slow them down," he said.

"Carefully," Nadia said. "Please."

"Always."

He ended the call, clipped the phone to his vest, and moved.

* * *

The crater floor was lower than the surrounding terrain by perhaps forty feet, accessed from the south by a scramble route that technical hikers used in daylight. In the dark, at speed, it was a different proposition — loose rock, drop-offs that you only understood if you'd walked them before, angled slabs that looked flat and were not. Dax had walked them before, many times, enough times that his body knew the path before his eyes confirmed it. He came off the rim in a controlled descent, paws finding each surface with the automatic certainty of muscle memory, and hit the crater floor at a run.

The three figures were at the vehicle now. He heard the heavy thud of the vehicle's rear cargo door swinging shut, heard the first engine note turning over.

"Hey."

His voice carried cleanly in the cold desert air. All three figures turned.

The one with the headlamp — a broad man with a close-cut beard showing gray at the edges — swung the light toward him and held it there. Dax didn't squint. He simply looked back, letting them see what they were seeing: a cheetah in a carbon-fiber vest, gold eyes bright in the lamp's beam, standing in the crater floor with his weight shifted slightly forward.

The broad man didn't seem confused or alarmed by this, which was itself alarming. He'd been prepared for the possibility.

"This is private research," the broad man said. His voice had the flat, managed quality of someone who had practiced that specific sentence. "You're trespassing on a permitted site."

"There's no permit for what you're doing here," Dax said. "And this is public land. So we have an argument about facts before we have any other kind of argument." He let his eyes move briefly to the other two — a woman with her back against the vehicle, arms crossed, watching him with a careful stillness, and a younger man near the driver's side who had his hand resting at his hip in a way that was not casual. "I'm asking you to wait here until the park service arrives. That's the whole request. Just wait."

The broad man tilted his head. "I appreciate the civility," he said. "I genuinely do." He reached up and switched off his headlamp.

The crater went dark.

Dax's eyes adjusted in the half-second it took the human eye to begin its much longer adjustment process. He could see the broad man stepping left, the younger man moving away from the vehicle toward the passenger side, the woman pushing off from the door and angling right. Three directions, practiced, the geometry of a play they'd rehearsed.

The vehicle's engine caught and held.

Dax moved.

He came in at an angle across the crater floor that brought him parallel with the vehicle rather than in front of it — not blocking, not confronting, but closing distance. His left forepaw hit the rock and the familiar pulse of warmth moved up through it, that deep internal light responding to urgency the way it always did, and the golden glow briefly lit the ground beneath him in a moving trail of fading prints as he accelerated.

The younger man stepped into his path.

He was fast for a human, genuinely fast, and the motion was professional — low center of gravity, weight on the balls of his feet, something in his right hand catching a gleam of moonlight. Dax had about a third of a second to make a decision.

He made it by going lower and faster simultaneously, dropping his chest nearly to the stone and pushing off with both hindlegs in a burst that carried him under the man's reach and past him before contact was made. He felt the air of a swinging arm pass over his back. He came up on the other side, spun, and was facing the vehicle again.

The broad man had vanished into the dark. The woman was already at the passenger door.

The vehicle began to roll.

Dax put himself in front of it.

Not as a gamble — he was not in the habit of gambling with tons of moving machinery — but as a calculation. The bore-hole was behind him. The vehicle couldn't go around him without crossing the uneven ground near the crater's upthrust center. It could go through him, but that required a driver willing to do that, and in Dax's experience, most people in most situations were not willing to do that. Not when they could see your face clearly and the golden light was pulsing under your paws and the glow of fourteen footprints still faded slowly in the rock behind you.

The vehicle stopped.

Six feet of illuminated headlights and engine noise, stopped.

Dax held very still and looked at the windshield. Through the glass he could see the driver's face — a fourth figure, someone who had been in the vehicle all along, someone he hadn't counted. Younger than the others, with the wide-set eyes of someone calculating odds.

He heard, faintly, the sound of a helicopter beginning somewhere to the south. Nadia. Or more precisely: whoever Nadia had called who had a helicopter. The sound was still distant, still only a suggestion in the lower frequencies, but it was there.

The driver heard it too.

Their eyes met through the glass — a cheetah's gold eyes and a human's uncertain ones — and for a long moment the plateau held its breath around them. The night insects were still absent. The river was still far below. The ancient rock held the warmth of the day in its million-year patience.

Then the driver's door opened.

The fourth figure stepped out with their hands open at their sides, empty, and looked at Dax across the hood of the vehicle with an expression that carried something unexpected: not defeat, not anger, but a complicated and almost curious assessment, as though this outcome had been one of the scenarios they'd modeled but had given lower probability than it deserved.

"The cylinders stay," Dax said.

The driver nodded once, slowly. "The cylinders stay."

Behind him, somewhere in the dark of the crater, the broad man with the gray beard had gone silent in a way that felt deliberate, patient, and not particularly surrendering.

The helicopter sound grew.

Chapter Two

One Unaccounted For

The helicopter announced itself properly two minutes later — a searchlight sweeping across the crater's pale upper rim before the sound of the rotors had fully separated itself from the wind. It was a National Park Service aircraft, a Bell 407 that Dax recognized by its silhouette and lighting configuration, which meant Nadia had reached exactly the right person at exactly the right hour, a small miracle of persistence that he'd learned to take for granted.

The searchlight found the vehicle. Then it found Dax. Then, methodically, it began to quarter the crater floor.

The fourth figure — the driver, still standing with open hands beside the hood — flinched at the light but did not move. Dax watched him from twelve feet away with the particular attention he gave to people who had chosen stillness over motion. Most people, flooded with a helicopter searchlight in a national park at eleven at night, moved. They shielded their eyes, turned away, shifted their weight toward some instinctive idea of escape. This man stood with a flatness to his posture that suggested he had already finished his internal argument and arrived at a conclusion. Dax had seen that posture before. He was less certain of what it meant here.

The woman was still at the passenger door. The younger man who had come at him — fast, trained, the gleam of something in his hand that Dax had not waited to identify — had moved to the far side of the vehicle and stopped there, which was either a decision about optics or a decision about positioning, and Dax was not yet sure which.

The broad man with the gray beard had not reappeared.

This fact occupied the back of his mind with the low, continuous attention he gave to incomplete arithmetic.

The helicopter circled once, the searchlight painting the twisted upthrust rock at the crater's center in moving white geometry, and then it began to descend toward the flat ground two hundred yards south. The rotors kicked up a ringing curtain of dust and grit that swept across the crater floor and Dax turned his face into it without flinching, squinting to keep his eyes clear, ears flat. The dust tasted of sandstone and something older — the deep-mineral cold of Permian rock, the Organ Rock Shale that formed the crater's exposed heart, deposited when this ground had been an inland sea in the time before any animal alive today could have looked at it and called it familiar.

The rotors slowed. Three figures came out of the aircraft at a purposeful walk.

Two were park rangers — he recognized one of them, a tall Diné woman named Begay who ran the Island in the Sky district, someone he'd worked alongside during a flash-flood rescue the previous spring. The third was a man in civilian clothing carrying a case, moving with the careful economy of someone who'd been pulled from bed and had decided to be professional about it.

Ranger Begay's flashlight swept across the scene. It landed on the vehicle, the canvas-covered cylinders in the flatbed, the steel casing stacked near the bore-hole. Then it found Dax.

"GoldenPaw," she said. Not a greeting exactly. More like a confirmation of a hypothesis.

"Ranger Begay." He took two steps back from the vehicle, making the geometry of the scene cleaner for whoever was going to have to interpret it later. "Four individuals. A vehicle with no visible rear plate. Fourteen cylinders, canvas-covered, contents unknown, pulled from that shaft." He nodded toward the bore-hole casing. "Three individuals visible. One unaccounted for, somewhere in the crater. Armed with at least one unknown implement. They have not been cooperative and they have not been violent."

"Yet," said the younger man from the far side of the vehicle, and there was something in his voice that was more resignation than threat.

Ranger Begay walked to the flatbed and lifted a corner of the canvas. She looked at the cylinders for a moment without touching them. The man with the case came up beside her, set it down, and opened it. He had the instruments of someone who analyzed things. Dax filed him as EPA but did not say so.

"You drill this?" Begay asked the fourth figure — the driver.

"I'd like to speak with counsel," the driver said. Clear, calm, entirely prepared.

"That's fine," Begay said, in a tone that indicated she'd expected nothing else. "You can do that at the station. Right now I need everyone to move away from the vehicle."

The woman at the passenger door moved. The younger man moved. The driver moved. They did all of this with the studied cooperation of people who had decided, at some prior and collective moment, that resistance at this particular stage served no one. Which meant that the current situation was not the only one they had prepared for, and that whatever came next had already been considered.

Dax moved to the rim of the bore-hole.

The EPA man was already beside him, running a probe on a short cable down into the shaft. He read the display for a moment, and then he read it again, and then he looked up at Dax with the particular expression of a person whose professional certainty has been briefly and completely upended.

"What," Dax said.

"The SO2 reading is incidental," the EPA man said. "Outgassing from the drilling process, basically. That's the sulfur you smelled. But the primary compound they were collecting — " He turned the display so Dax could see the readout, though the numbers meant less to Dax than the tone in which the man had delivered the preceding sentence. "This is a helium-3 concentration that I can't explain without — " He stopped. Started again. "Helium-3 doesn't occur in these concentrations in sedimentary formations. It's a primordial isotope. You find it in undifferentiated mantle material or in — " He paused again, gestured vaguely upward, which in context meant not sky but beyond sky. "Or in high-energy impact melt. The kind deposited by a bolide event."

The crater. The upthrust white rock at its center. Half a billion years of argument about what had made this place, and someone had apparently settled that argument at least partially, quietly, with a drill and a set of pressurized cylinders and no permit.

"They found something in the impact layer," Dax said.

"They found a pocket of something in the impact layer," the EPA man corrected, and there was a precision to the distinction that carried weight. "Something that has been sealed in the lowest strata since the impact event. Sixty million years, minimum. And they knew exactly where to look."

Dax turned that over. Knowing exactly where to look, in a crater five miles across, in a national park, at night, with equipment that had been transported cross-country and would leave no trace — that was not the work of opportunity. That was the endpoint of a long chain of prior knowledge, and prior knowledge of that specificity had a source.

He looked at the three figures now standing in a loose group under Ranger Begay's flashlight. The driver, the woman, the younger man. All of them with the composed blankness of people who were already thinking about next steps rather than about the current one.

He thought about the fourth figure. The broad man who had switched off his headlamp and walked into the dark with the patience of someone who didn't expect to be caught in the same net as the others.

"Begay," he said.

She was already looking at him. She had been the kind of ranger who looked everywhere at once for eleven years and it showed.

"One of them is still in the crater," he said. "I don't know where."

She gave a short nod, touched her radio. "I've got a second aircraft coming up from Moab, should be here in twenty minutes. We'll do a full sweep." She glanced at the crater walls — the pale Navajo Sandstone rising in broken shelves toward the rim, the dark fissures between rock faces where a person who knew what they were doing could become very small and very still. "Assuming he hasn't already climbed out."

That was the calculation, and it was a sound one. The crater rim was forty feet of rough but climbable sandstone. In the time since the helicopter had arrived, with its searchlight committed to the crater floor, the broad man could have gone up and over and into the surrounding plateau at any compass point. The plateau was not large by geological standards, but it was a serious and committed wilderness, and a person with good preparation and no particular interest in being found could walk several directions from here and find no roads for a long time.

Dax looked at the cylinder nearest him. Through the canvas he could feel the faint cold of pressurized metal. Sixty million years of trapped gas from a wound in the planet, drilled out and bottled in the dark by people who had taken months to plan this and minutes to almost succeed.

He pulled his attention back to the driver, who was watching him.

The fourth figure had that look again — the careful, almost scientific assessment. Dax had been looked at in a lot of ways since Brendan Cho's cell phone video had launched a hundred comment threads. With fear, with skepticism, with the slightly bewildered curiosity that most people settled into once they had confirmed he was real. This was none of those. This was the look of someone who had read a report about a thing and was now calibrating how accurate the report had been.

"You knew I'd be here," Dax said. Not loudly. Just across the eight feet of moonlit crater floor between them.

The driver's eyes shifted slightly. Not denial. Not confirmation. The shift of someone who had decided to give up nothing freely but who had not expected the question to arrive as precisely as it had.

"The timing," Dax said. "You were almost finished when I arrived. You had been here — " he looked at the disassembled wellhead casing, the deliberate efficiency of the cleanup — "three hours, minimum, maybe four. And you were almost gone. But you didn't make it. Which means your timeline was calibrated around something, and that something was wrong." He waited. "What was it calibrated around?"

The driver said nothing. But the woman, very briefly, looked away from Dax toward the crater rim — a quick, involuntary glance northeast, in the direction of nothing visible, that was over before Ranger Begay would have caught it.

Dax caught it.

He filed it the way he filed the broad man's disappearance: as information that was incomplete but would eventually resolve into something with shape.

"Alright," Ranger Begay said, moving the group toward the aircraft. "Let's go."

The three figures walked. The EPA man began photographing the cylinders with methodical care. The helicopter's rotors were at idle speed, barely stirring the dust now. In the vast surrounding dark, the canyon country lay in its ancient silence, the Green River two thousand feet below carrying its cold snowmelt south toward the Colorado, the rock walls holding the heat of the day in their Permian and Triassic patience.

Dax stayed at the bore-hole.

He crouched and looked down into the shaft. The probe's cable still hung in it. A few feet below the steel casing the rock changed color — from the buff and orange of the upper sandstone to something paler, almost white, the shatter-fractured heart of an impact that had reshaped this ground when the plateau did not yet exist, when the entire Rocky Mountain system was still flat sea floor, when whatever sky had covered this place looked out at a solar system that was still learning its own geometry.

Something down there, sealed for sixty million years. And someone had known it was there.

That was the part that stayed with him as the second helicopter arrived in a roar of rotors from the south, its searchlight beginning its methodical sweep of the crater walls and the rim and the long exposed sandstone benches beyond. The knowing. The particular, precise, actionable knowing that could put three people and a drill and fourteen cylinders into the exact right spot on five miles of tortured rock at night.

He stood. He looked northeast, in the direction the woman's eyes had gone.

The darkness offered him nothing. The night insects were beginning, tentatively, to return. Somewhere far down in the canyon a coyote spoke once and was answered by nothing.

Dax walked to the crater rim and climbed it in the dark, and when he reached the top he stood on the sandstone and looked out across the plateau in every direction. The second helicopter's searchlight was working the southwest. To the northeast, past the long pale fin of the Syncline Loop and the invisible thread of the trail that switchbacked down toward Upheaval Bottom, there was only the dark.

He brought out the satellite phone.

Nadia answered before it had finished its first tone. She had almost certainly been holding it.

"EPA is on site," he said. "Three in custody, one unaccounted for. Cylinders are intact."

"What's in them?"

"Helium-3. Or something with helium-3 as a component. From the impact layer."

A pause. He heard her breathing change slightly — the particular quality of silence from someone reassessing the shape of a thing they'd thought they understood. "Helium-3," she said.

"From sixty million years of sealed rock."

Another pause, longer. "That has applications," she said carefully.

"I know."

"Fusion research. Advanced propulsion. A handful of defense programs I can't name specifically because I've only read around the edges of them."

"I know," he said again. He was looking northeast, along the dark ridge, where the plateau stretched away in rolling fins of sandstone and juniper shadow toward no road and no light. "Nadia. Someone gave them the location. Someone with specific geological intelligence about what was under this crater and where to drill for it."

"That's a short list," she said.

"Yes."

"Academic. Federal. Private."

"One of those," he agreed. He watched the dark. The coyote had gone quiet. The second helicopter's light moved slowly southwest, finding nothing. "The man who ran — the one we haven't found. He's the one who ran the operation. The others were professionals but they were executing a plan, not making one. He made it."

"And he's out there somewhere."

"He walked out," Dax said. "He had his exit ready before the helicopter arrived. That means he had a way out of the park that doesn't use any road the rangers know to watch." He paused. "I want to find him before he reaches it."

Chapter Three

A Memorized Route

"Be careful," Nadia said, for the second time that night.

"Working on it," he said, and was already moving.

He went northeast at three-quarter speed, which on the open slickrock was fast enough to feel like flight without the recklessness that full sprint required in the dark. The plateau here was a succession of low fins and swales, the sandstone pale between patches of cryptobiotic crust that he avoided out of habit — the living soil crust, dark and fragile and centuries in the making, which left no track and asked only to be left alone. He honored that, even now. Especially now. The golden prints pulsed briefly behind each stride and then went dark.

He was tracking more with his nose than his eyes.

The broad man had been in the crater for several hours. You could not stand four feet from an open bore-shaft venting SO2 for four hours and walk away clean. The sulfur compound had penetrated his clothing, his skin, the close-cut gray beard, and it was moving northeast with him through the cold air like a signature. Dax let his nostrils work and reduced his conscious attention to a low hum and ran.

The plateau was not empty at this hour. It was never empty if you knew how to read it. A nighthawk cut above the junipers, evidence of normal air, no human disturbance in the direction it had come from. A jackrabbit crossed fifty meters ahead without the freeze-and-bolt that meant recent close passage. He adjusted his bearing slightly south of due northeast, reading the absence of disturbance the way he might read a map.

Then the wind shifted and gave him confirmation.

He was close.

He slowed to a walk, placing his paws with the deliberate silence of a cheetah that had grown up understanding that the distance between hunter and hunted collapses most usefully when the prey does not yet know it has happened. The rock here was ribbed and broken, low shelves of Kayenta sandstone dropping in irregular steps toward a long shallow draw choked with juniper. Below the draw, invisible in the dark, the land fell away in the long grades that led eventually to the canyon systems feeding Upheaval Bottom.

The broad man was in the junipers.

Not hiding — moving, making time, threading the trees with the competence of someone who had walked this exact route before and likely at night. Dax could hear him now: the soft compression of old juniper needles underfoot, the occasional brush of a low branch, the controlled breathing of someone in good physical condition managing a purposeful pace.

He had a headlamp he wasn't using.

That was interesting. That meant he had either memorized the route well enough to walk it dark, or he had a different kind of light source — something infrared, something that operated on a spectrum that human eyes couldn't catch. Dax's eyes were not human eyes. He watched the man's shape moving through the junipers thirty meters ahead: dark clothing, a pack he had not had in the crater, which meant he had staged it out here beforehand, had cached it in the dark plateau before walking in to run the extraction operation.

Months of planning. The pack confirmed months.

Dax angled ahead and to the right, using the longer legs and the speed to place himself quietly at the far end of the draw before the man emerged from the junipers. He settled into a crouch behind a low sandstone shelf and waited.

The footsteps came on, steady and unhurried. The broad man came out of the junipers and paused, his face turning once toward the south — toward the distant pulse of the second helicopter's searchlight, still working the crater's southwest quadrant — and then he turned forward and saw Dax.

They regarded each other across ten feet of bare rock.

The broad man was calm. Dax had expected this. He was the kind of person whose response to the unexpected was a slight recalibration rather than a visible reaction, and Dax had suspected since the moment the headlamp went off that this was true of him. He set the pack down with deliberate care, neither surrendering it nor reaching into it. A man making a statement about his intentions.

"You're faster than the report indicated," he said.

"What report," Dax said.

The broad man smiled, very slightly. Not warmly. The smile of someone making a note. "There's always a report."

The helicopter sound swelled briefly and then diminished to the south. The coyote had gone silent again. Somewhere below, the river moved in its ancient course, carrying snowmelt from mountains the broad man could not see and Dax knew by heart.

"The helium-3," Dax said. "You knew what was down there. That means you had survey data, and survey data of that specificity doesn't come from a standard geological assessment. Someone gave it to you. Or you took it."

The broad man looked at him for a moment with the careful attention he'd been applying to everything since Dax had first called out in the crater. "I took nothing that wasn't going to be buried for another sixty million years," he said. "That's not a legal argument. I understand that. But it's the one that feels accurate."

"Who told you where to drill."

"That's a conversation for a different venue."

"The rangers are going to cover the roads out of the park," Dax said. "Whatever exit you cached — vehicle, aircraft, whatever is at the end of this route you've got memorized — they'll find it. You've got maybe thirty minutes before this plateau has more light on it than you want."

"Forty-five," the man said, with the mild precision of someone correcting a small error. He glanced southeast, some inner calculation visible briefly in his face. "But you're not wrong about the direction of travel."

Dax watched him. The pack sat between them on the stone. Whatever was in it mattered to the man enough to cache it out here, to risk the extra time, to plan the route around it. Not the cylinders — those were in the flatbed, those were already gone. This was something else.

"There's another bore-hole," Dax said. It came to him the way things did when he stopped trying to reach for them and simply let the accumulated facts arrange themselves. The prior knowledge. The timeline. The woman's eyes going northeast. The pack cached in the dark, heavy enough that the man had set it down with both hands. "You weren't here for the first extraction. You were here to confirm what the first extraction left behind, so the second one can be targeted better. The cylinders were a proof of concept."

The broad man was still for a moment. Then: "You're fast and you're not stupid. That combination is inconvenient."

"Where is it."

"You'll find it," the man said, and there was something in his voice that Dax took a moment to classify: not defiance, not resignation, but a strange and almost reluctant approval, the tone of a teacher who has just watched a student arrive at the correct answer through an unexpected method. "You'll find it and it will raise questions you haven't had to ask yet. About what's in the impact layer. About what a bolide of that mass brings with it across sixty million years of deep space." He paused. "About what it may have done to the geology of this place in ways that aren't strictly geological."

Dax looked at him steadily. "You're going to want to explain that."

"Not tonight." He picked the pack up again. "Tonight I'm going to walk to the north rim and you're going to let me, because the alternative is a confrontation that goes in a direction neither of us wants at this hour. I'm not carrying anything that belongs to the park. The cylinders are on the flatbed. Whatever judgments get made from here belong to people with different authorities than either of us." He held Dax's gaze. "But I'll tell you this much for free: the mine shaft under Manti-La Sal. You've been carrying that incident for eighteen months without an explanation. There's one. The impact layer at Upheaval Dome is part of it."

The night held still around them. The distant rotors. The cold river two thousand feet below. The sandstone holding its Permian patience in every direction.

Dax said: "What's your name."

The broad man considered this. "Harlan," he said. "That's real. The rest is complicated."

"Harlan." Dax looked at the pack, at the man's face, at the dark plateau stretching north toward no road and whatever waited at the end of a memorized route. He thought about Nadia's voice when he'd said sixty million years of sealed rock. He thought about the EPA man's expression at the readout. He thought about the thing he had not shown Nadia on the tablet the day he'd clocked himself on the hardpan north of Dead Horse Point.

"Go," Dax said.

Harlan nodded once — the same single nod the driver had given over the hood of the vehicle, and Dax understood now that it was a house style, a practiced acknowledgment shared among people who operated in a particular register. Harlan turned north and walked into the dark, the pack settled on his shoulders, his footsteps already quieting on the far stone.

Dax stayed on the low shelf until the sound was gone entirely.

Then he sat, the way he sometimes sat at the mesa edge and watched the river. The golden light pulsed once, faintly, under the pads of his forepaws and then went quiet. The second helicopter's searchlight was moving now toward the northeast quadrant, minutes too late, covering ground that would give it nothing useful.

He pulled out the satellite phone.

Nadia picked up.

"I found him," Dax said. "He's gone."

A pause. "Gone how."

"Gone deliberately." He looked north, where the dark plateau offered nothing but the slow wheel of the stars and the cold mineral patience of the oldest rock in the American Southwest. "Nadia. The mine. Manti-La Sal. I need you to pull everything you can find on the geological survey of that area. Not the standard USGS records. Whatever exists underneath the standard records."

The silence that followed had a different quality than her other silences — focused, already searching, the silence of a person whose hands had gone to a keyboard before the sentence was finished.

"Alright," she said.

"And the impact layer here. Whatever research exists on what a bolide of Upheaval's size would carry in its mass. What it might leave behind that isn't rock."

"Dax." Her voice was careful. "Are you okay?"

He looked down at his forepaws. No light now. Just the spotted fur and the dark stone and the ancient cooling night.

"I think I'm going to find out what I am," he said.

The river moved below, as it always had. The plateau held its breath. Somewhere north of him, a man named Harlan walked a memorized route toward an exit no ranger would find in time, carrying in his pack whatever the second bore-hole had been meant to illuminate, moving with the patience of someone who had already decided that this was not an ending but a middle — a single night in a long chain of prior knowledge that ran deeper than the rock, that had been accumulating since before the mountain system existed, since before any animal alive today could have looked at this sky and called it familiar.

Dax clipped the phone to his vest and sat for another moment in the dark, the plateau enormous and quiet around him, and let the shape of the thing settle into its new dimensions.

Then he stood, and the golden light rose briefly under his paws, and he turned south toward the crater and the cylinders and the work that had a name.
