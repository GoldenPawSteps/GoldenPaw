Chapter One

The Storm Window

The wind had teeth at twenty-three thousand feet.

It came screaming off the Annapurna massif without warning, as it always did, hurling curtains of loose snow sideways across the ridge like the exhalation of some indifferent god. Below, the village of Thonak — a scatter of whitewashed stone houses clinging to the mountain's flank like barnacles on the hull of a vast, ancient ship — disappeared entirely behind the whiteout. Above, there was nothing but gray sky and the suggestion of a summit that had swallowed whole expeditions and forgotten them.

And in between, moving at a speed that no living thing had any right to move at this altitude, was GoldenPaw.

He hit the ridgeline at full stride, four massive paws finding purchase on wind-scoured granite with an ease that would have baffled any mountaineer who happened to witness it — not that any could have kept their eyes focused on something moving that fast. His body was a long, muscular streak against the white and gray of the mountain, the distinctive amber and black rosette pattern of his coat vivid even in the flat, diffuse light of the storm. The golden bracers on his wrists caught what little sun filtered through the overcast and threw it back as warm, honeyed light. His amber eyes — wide-set, sharp as the mountain's own ridges — scanned the terrain below with the absolute, unhurried focus that was his most dangerous quality.

Not speed. Not strength. Focus.

He dropped from the ridge onto a near-vertical ice face and simply ran down it, claws extending instinctively with each footfall, punching through the surface crust just enough for grip before pulling free in the next stride. The wind screamed. He leaned into it.

Dawa. The name had been in his head since Kathmandu, since the frantic call from the mountain rescue coordination office. Twelve years old. Separated from a school group on the Annapurna Circuit above Thorong La. Storm window closing in forty minutes. Maybe less.

Forty minutes had been twenty minutes ago.

He cleared a buttress of black rock in a single, arcing leap that carried him forty feet through the howling air, landed in a deep drift that would have swallowed a person to the waist, and was through it and moving again before the displaced snow had finished settling. His nose was already working — cutting through the cold mineral bite of the altitude and the clean, nothing smell of fresh snow — searching for the warm, frightened, living scent of a child.

The Himalayas did not care about children. They did not care about anything. That was the first thing GoldenPaw had understood when he had come here, three years ago, following a thread of instinct and old grief up from the sun-baked plains and into these impossible, indifferent heights. The mountains were not cruel. They were simply complete. They required nothing and offered nothing, and everything that lived in their shadow did so entirely on its own terms.

He had made his peace with that.

A shift in the wind. A thermal rolling up from the valley, carrying with it the ghost of a charcoal fire, yak butter, wool — and beneath all of that, faint and ragged and terrified, the scent of a small human being.

GoldenPaw changed direction without breaking stride, forty degrees to the east, pushing harder now, the muscles in his haunches firing in long, explosive waves. His breath came in rhythmic, visible clouds. The storm tightened around him like a fist.

Hold on, Dawa, he thought, though there was no one to hear it. Just hold on.

Chapter Two

A Good Fire

He found the boy the way he found most things in the mountains — not by looking, but by listening.

The granite overhang was barely visible in the whiteout, a dark slit in the cliff face twenty feet above the scree field, accessible by a narrow ledge that a mountain goat would have thought twice about. But the scent was stronger now, close enough to separate into its components: woodsmoke from a fire someone small and resourceful had managed to coax into existence, damp wool, the specific sharp-sweet smell of fear metabolizing into something closer to exhaustion.

GoldenPaw took the ledge in two bounds and ducked beneath the overhang.

The boy was sitting with his back against the stone, knees drawn to his chest, a small fire burning in a ring of rocks before him — feeding it, GoldenPaw noted, with the cardboard backing torn from a notebook, which was the kind of practical thinking that kept people alive up here. He was perhaps twelve, dark-eyed, wearing a red jacket that had been doing its considerable best against the cold. His face, when he looked up, cycled through several expressions in quick succession: terror, recognition, and then something that was not quite relief but was reaching toward it.

"GoldenPaw," the boy said. His voice came out smaller than he probably intended.

"Dawa." GoldenPaw settled back on his haunches, keeping his posture deliberately low, unthreatening. His breath steamed in the cold air between them. "You built a good fire."

The boy looked at the small, struggling flames. "I read about it," he said. "In a book."

"Good book."

Outside, the storm pressed hard against the mountain's flank, indifferent as always.

Chapter Three

The Fox

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fire popped. A gust found the overhang's mouth and the flames leaned sideways, then steadied.

GoldenPaw let the silence do its work. He had learned, over years in these mountains and the lowland places before them, that silence after fear was not emptiness. It was something a person needed to move through at their own pace, like a river ford. You did not rush it.

He studied the boy without appearing to. Dawa's fingers, he noted, were cold but not white — no frostbite yet. His breathing had slowed from the ragged hitch it must have been an hour ago into something approaching normal. The red jacket was soaked through at the shoulders but the rest of him had stayed dry under the overhang. Twelve years old and he had found shelter, built a fire, and kept himself awake. That was not luck. That was the particular stubbornness that the mountains either killed or made permanent.

"Where were you when you got separated?" GoldenPaw asked. His voice was low, conversational, the same tone he might use discussing the weather.

Dawa thought about it seriously, the way children did when they had decided to be honest. "The Thorong La side. Below the cairns. The group stopped and I — " He paused. "I went to look at something."

"What something?"

The boy met his eyes for the first time. "A fox. It was just sitting there in the snow."
