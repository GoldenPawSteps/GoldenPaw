Chapter One

Off the Trail

The wind had no mercy at 5,400 metres.

It came off the Tibetan plateau like a living thing — a scouring, howling wall of ice-edged air that tore at every exposed surface and turned the ridgeline into a blade. Snow devils spiralled up from the corniced edge of the pass and dissolved into the bruised-grey sky. Below, in the deep throat of the valley, the lights of the village of Thyangoche flickered like the last embers of a dying fire.

GoldenPaw didn't feel the cold the way a man would. His body — lean and long-limbed, wrapped in a pelt of tawny gold broken by solid black spots — was built for speed, not warmth. Still, he crouched low behind a spur of black rock and let the gust rip overhead, his amber eyes narrowing against the sting of ice crystals. He pressed one broad, padded hand flat against the stone to steady himself, the semi-retractable claws clicking faintly against the surface. Beneath the fingers, the mountain trembled.

Not from wind. From something below.

He tilted his head. The tear-mark stripes running from the inner corners of his eyes down to the edges of his jaw — dark as charcoal, sharp as the creases of a permanent worry — caught the last grey light of the fading afternoon. His nostrils flared, processing the high thin air in one long, deliberate breath. Snow. Pine resin from the forest three thousand metres below. The acrid bite of burning kerosene.

And something else. Something chemical and wrong.

He had been following the signal for six hours. Not a digital signal — the device clipped beneath his left forearm, half-hidden by the short flight-suit of deep amber and black that he wore over his natural markings, had gone dark just past the Nangpa La trail. What he followed now was older than any satellite technology. It was the particular tension in the air that preceded catastrophe, the same pressure-change intuition that had pulled him out of bed in Kathmandu at three in the morning with every hair along his spine standing stiff and straight.

Something was wrong in Thyangoche.

He rose from behind the rock in a single fluid motion. The wind hit him full across the chest and he leaned into it, unimpressed, the flexible spine that gave his species its legendary bounding stride absorbing the lateral push with a casual adjustment of his hips. He was not especially large, as far as anthropomorphic felines went. He stood roughly two metres at his full height, with a greyhound's economical build — all clean muscle laid over a frame that looked almost fragile until you watched it move. What he lacked in bulk he more than compensated for in raw velocity. On flat ground, on a good surface, he had clocked himself at one hundred and twelve kilometres per hour. Even here, on broken high-altitude terrain, he moved at speeds that made conventional pursuit pointless.

He studied the descent ahead. The trail down to Thyangoche was a zigzagging grey scar cut across an almost vertical face of shale and frozen scree, switchbacking through two lower bands of rhododendron thicket before opening onto the narrow stone-flagged lanes of the village. In the dying afternoon light, the whole face of the mountain was a study in blue shadow and dirty white. He could see figures moving on the lowest stretch of trail. Four of them. Moving upward, quickly, carrying something between them.

He pulled a small monocular from the suit's chest pocket and pressed it to one eye.

The figures were men. They wore heavy cold-weather gear — not the worn, practical layering of the Sherpa villagers he had passed earlier that day, but uniform black thermal suits that spoke of money and intention. Three of them carried long, hard-sided cases. The fourth was in front, moving with the tight-jointed efficiency of someone who had trained for hostile terrain. He was directing the others with short chopping gestures of one gloved hand.

The one at the rear was watching the village below. Watching it the way a man watches something he has already decided to leave behind.

GoldenPaw lowered the monocular and his jaw tightened. The black tear marks on his face deepened as his expression shifted — not into anger, exactly, but into the particular cold, clarifying focus that replaced anger when the situation demanded precision over emotion. He had worn that expression a great deal over the past two years.

He had come to Nepal three weeks ago at the request of no one. No government ministry, no international task force, no shadowy consortium of well-meaning humans had sent him. He had come because a series of data fragments — scraped from disparate sources, assembled at four in the morning on a laptop in a rented room in Pokhara — had pointed toward the Khumbu region with the quiet insistence of a fist tapping on a door. Mineral survey data filed under false geological registrations. Procurement records for cold-climate drilling equipment that had moved through Kathmandu customs under the designation of scientific research apparatus. Three missing persons reports from villages within a forty-kilometre radius, each involving men who had worked as seasonal high-altitude porters.

None of it, individually, was alarming. Stacked together and viewed from a certain angle, it assembled into something that made the fur along his collar ridge stand up.

The wind dropped for a moment. In the sudden relative quiet, a sound drifted up from the valley — a low, resonant concussive thud, like a fist striking a very large drum from the inside.

Then another.

Then, from the direction of the village, a sound he hadn't expected at this distance: a child's voice, high and piercing, cut abruptly short.

GoldenPaw was already moving.

He went down the first fifty metres of trail in four seconds, his paws finding the iced shale with the instinctive sureness of an animal designed by evolution to pursue and correct, pursue and correct, each stride a tiny act of balance and recalibration. The cold-weather suit's panels flexed with him, the amber-and-black colouration rendering him, in the fading light, almost indistinguishable from the mottled rock face. His tail streamed out behind him, a thick, ringed rudder making infinitesimal adjustments as he carved through the first switchback without reducing speed.

The four figures on the lower trail had not yet seen him. They were focused downward, on whatever the thudding sound had signalled. The lead man had stopped and was pressing a hand to his ear — a comm device, GoldenPaw noted. He was receiving instructions from someone who was not on this mountain face.

Which meant there were more of them, somewhere below.

He slowed at the second switchback, pressed himself against the rock wall, and calculated. The men were perhaps three hundred metres below him now, on a trail section that cut back horizontally across the face before descending again. If he continued straight down the main trail, he would emerge onto that horizontal section roughly forty metres ahead of them. They would hear him long before he arrived. The element of surprise — the only tactical advantage he reliably possessed against armed opponents — would evaporate.

He looked at the rock face to his left. Sheer, frost-blackened, broken by a series of narrow ledges that a snow leopard might have navigated with some confidence and a human climber would have required anchors, ropes, and considerable optimism to attempt.

GoldenPaw studied it for two seconds. Then he stepped off the trail.

His claws found the first ledge by touch in the dark. His fingers wrapped around a handhold that was barely wider than two of his fingertips. He moved laterally across the face with the fluid, unhurried confidence of something that simply does not entertain the possibility of falling — not because it is reckless, but because the body calculates the geometry of grip and balance so quickly and completely that falling never enters the decision tree.

Below, in the village, the thudding sounded once more. Closer, somehow. Or louder.

He moved faster.

Chapter Two

The Beneath

The lead operative heard him first.

Not the footfall — GoldenPaw had landed soundlessly on the packed snow of the canyon floor, thirty metres behind the group, knees absorbing the drop from the ledge with a compression that would have shattered a human ankle. What the man heard was the displacement of air. A pressure change. The subtle wrongness that the hindbrain interprets, before the conscious mind catches up, as something is behind me.

He turned a half-second too late.

GoldenPaw came out of the shadow at full sprint. The distance closed in the time it took the operative to open his mouth to shout a warning. He was a large man — broad through the shoulders, with the settled weight of someone who had spent years accumulating professional competence — and he planted his feet and reached for the sidearm holstered at his left hip with the practiced economy of long training.

He never completed the draw.

GoldenPaw hit him low and off-centre, one hand stripping the weapon from its holster with a flick of the wrist as his shoulder drove into the man's sternum, and then they were both in motion — the operative backwards, GoldenPaw already redirecting, his momentum carrying him clear of the falling body and into the space where the second man stood. The second man had the presence of mind to swing one of the hard-sided cases as an improvised weapon. GoldenPaw ducked under it, felt the displaced air ruffle the fur along the top of his skull, and came up inside the man's reach with an elbow strike that clicked the man's teeth together and folded him at the knees.

Two seconds. Two down.

The third and fourth men had dropped their cases and separated, moving in opposite directions to bracket him. This, GoldenPaw noted with the detached analytical register that sat beneath his tactical awareness like a running commentary, was practised. Not the improvised response of frightened men. They had drilled for a fast attacker. Someone had anticipated that a fast attacker was a possibility.

That information tucked itself away in the back of his mind to be examined later.

He did not let them complete the bracket. He broke left — toward the man who was moving faster, who represented the greater immediate threat — and covered the distance in a single explosive stride, catching the operative's outstretched arm at the wrist, redirecting the force of the man's own charge into a rotation that put him face-down in the snow. The impact knocked the wind from him in a single sharp exhalation.

The fourth man had a weapon up now. Something compact and dark, pressed into his shoulder, the barrel tracking with professional steadiness. He was a measured distance back — twenty metres — too far for GoldenPaw to close before the man could fire.

GoldenPaw stopped. His tail moved once, slowly, behind him.

The operative's eyes, visible above a heavy balaclava, were pale and very calm. He said nothing. He waited.

“Your friends are still breathing,” GoldenPaw said. His voice was even, slightly rough, carrying the faint residual accent of a childhood spent between two languages. “I'd like to keep it that way on both sides. Put it down.”

The pale eyes did not move. Neither did the barrel.

“There's a village burning below us,” GoldenPaw continued. “Whatever is happening down there is the part I'm interested in. You four are an obstacle I'd prefer to step around, if you'll let me.”

A pause. Wind moved between them. Then the operative's earpiece crackled — GoldenPaw could hear it at this distance, a tinny, compressed burst of sound — and something shifted in the pale eyes. Not fear. Something more complex. Something that looked almost like resignation.

The man lowered the weapon. Not fully — it dropped to a low ready, pointed at the ground between them — but the message was clear enough.

“Move past,” the man said. His accent placed him somewhere in the former Soviet sphere. Georgia, possibly, or Armenia. “We are not your problem tonight.”

GoldenPaw looked at him for a long moment. Then at the three cases lying abandoned in the snow. He crossed to the nearest one in three strides, flipped the latches, and looked inside.

Survey equipment. Seismic sensors, tubular and matte-grey, nested in cut foam. A portable signal unit, military-grade, with a satellite uplink antenna folded flat against its housing. And, at the bottom of the case, wrapped in a soft cloth that had been hastily and inadequately re-secured, four cylindrical devices roughly the size and shape of thermoses, each with a blinking amber status light.

Remote detonators. Planted charges, and the means to trigger them.

GoldenPaw straightened slowly. He looked back at the operative with the weapon at low ready. The man's calm had not shifted. His was the posture of someone who has concluded that honesty, at this particular moment, is tactically preferable to resistance.

“How many charges?” GoldenPaw asked.

A pause. “Below the village. The subsurface.”

“How many people are down there?”

The pale eyes moved for the first time — a fractional glance to the left, the involuntary tell of someone constructing an answer from uncomfortable components.

“How many?” GoldenPaw repeated, and this time the evenness of his voice had a different quality to it. The same surface stillness, but with something underneath that was very much alive.

“We were told the evacuation was complete.” The man's voice had acquired the careful cadence of a statement being given rather than a conversation being held. “We were told this before we came up.”

GoldenPaw looked down at the valley for a fraction of a second. The kerosene smell was stronger now. So was the chemical smell he had noted from the ridge — sharper at lower altitude, resolving into something more specific. Ammonium nitrate. Explosive precursor. And beneath that, something more acrid, something that set off receptors in his nasal passages that he associated with serious, systemic wrongness.

He closed the case, straightened, and looked back at the operative.

“Don't follow me,” he said. “And don't touch those detonators.”

He left them there in the canyon and moved toward the village.

* * *

The stone wall at the northern edge of Thyangoche had a hole in it. Not a dramatic, cinematic explosion-hole — it had been opened with something measured and purposeful, a tight concentrated charge applied to a specific section of the base course, leaving the upper courses intact but unsupported so that they had sagged inward rather than outward. An engineer's work. Someone who wanted access, not spectacle.

GoldenPaw slipped through the gap and came down into the narrow lane beyond.

The village was older than most of what surrounded it — stone buildings of the classic Khumbu pattern, their thick walls painted in faded ochre and white, wooden window frames carved with geometric patterns worn smooth by altitude and time. In the ordinary run of events it would be lit at this hour by the warm amber glow of yak-butter lamps, the smell of evening cooking drifting from every second doorway, children's voices mixing with the distant bell-tone of the monastery that sat on the small rise at the village's eastern edge.

The monastery bell was ringing now, but not in any pattern that suggested ceremony.

It was ringing because someone was pulling the rope continuously, urgently, without rhythm.

GoldenPaw moved down the lane in a low run, back pressed to the right-hand wall, reading the scene ahead. Two buildings on the northern side of the central square were burning — not structurally, but their ground-floor contents had been set alight and the smoke was thick and black, pouring from the window openings and filling the square with a low, rolling opacity. It smelled of synthetic material. Equipment. The kind of fire that happens when you torch the contents of a storage space rather than the building itself.

In the square, three people. An elderly man — grey-haired, wearing the quilted jacket and dark trousers of a Sherpa villager — was pressed against the prayer wheel wall at the square's far edge. A woman of perhaps forty, her hair loose and her expression the controlled mask of someone managing their own terror with ferocious discipline, stood in front of him with one arm out in a gesture that was simultaneously protective and restraining. And in front of her, on the ground, a boy of perhaps nine or ten years, sitting with his knees drawn to his chest, staring at nothing.

The child's silence was the thing that had reached GoldenPaw from the ridge. He understood it now. Not the silence of sleep or of calm, but the silence on the far side of a sound too large for a child to make twice.

Two more of the black-suited operatives stood at the mouth of the lane that led south. One of them held a device GoldenPaw did not immediately recognise — compact, tripod-mounted, with a directional array of sensors aimed at the ground. The other was watching the three villagers without particular attention, his weapon at the same low-ready that GoldenPaw had seen on the trail. Keeping the situation contained. Waiting.

GoldenPaw assessed the geometry of the square in the time it took him to move along the wall to the corner. Two opponents. Line of sight to the monk pulling the bell rope — visible in the low tower of the monastery, still hauling with the mechanical determination of someone who had assigned himself this task and intended to complete it until told otherwise. The sensor device on the tripod was the unknown quantity. He had not seen equipment like it before, which meant it was either very new or very specialised, and neither of those things was reassuring.

He came around the corner fast and low, closing on the nearer operative before the smoke and the failing light allowed for proper resolution. He stripped the weapon from the man's grip with one hand while his other arm locked around the man's throat from behind — not a choke, but a control point, using the operative's own body weight against his balance. The man went to his knees with a sound of involuntary surprise. GoldenPaw eased him down and held the pressure just long enough to ensure cooperation.

The second operative had the directional device in one hand and was reaching across his body for his holster with the other — an awkward cross-draw that the tripod was making difficult. GoldenPaw was on him before he resolved the geometry, took the sidearm cleanly, and put a flat hand against the man's chest to push him firmly back against the lane wall.

“Both of you,” GoldenPaw said, “stay here and stay down.”

He crossed the square to the woman and the elderly man. She watched him approach with those eyes that had been managing terror into something steelier — he could see the recalibration happening in her expression as she took in his height, his colouration, the amber-and-black suit, the two confiscated weapons that he held loosely at his sides before setting them on the ground.

“Are you hurt?” he asked. His Nepali was serviceable, if not eloquent.

She blinked. Then, apparently deciding that the question was genuine and the asker was not, at this particular moment, the worst thing in the square, she shook her head. “Not us.” She glanced down at the boy. “He is — he is frightened. Only frightened.” Her English was precise, deliberate, the kind learned from textbooks and refined by years of guiding foreign trekkers. “Are you who the bell is for?”

“No one sent for me,” GoldenPaw said. “I came on my own.”

The elderly man behind her said something in rapid Sherpa dialect, and the woman translated without looking away from GoldenPaw. “He says they took three men. From the village. They took Kanchha, Tashi, and Dorje. They went below — into the beneath.”

“The beneath,” GoldenPaw repeated.

She nodded. “There is a shaft. Two kilometres south, in the rock below the moraine. They have been — building it, we think. For many months. We saw the lights, sometimes, in the deep winter. The men who went to look did not come back.” She paused. “These three who were taken tonight — the men in black came to the houses, to the doors. They were looking for something. For someone. They asked about the shaft and Kanchha tried to send them away and they took him. Tashi and Dorje went after.”

The boy, without looking up, said something very quietly.

The woman's expression changed. Something crossed her face that was not fear and was not grief exactly — something that sat in the space between those two things.

“He says they had a box,” she said quietly. “A metal box. Kanchha was carrying it when they took him. He had been keeping it for two months, the boy says. He found it in the high meadow below the Khombu ice fall and he kept it. He told no one.” She looked down at the child. “He told the boy.”

GoldenPaw crouched, putting himself at the boy's eye level. The boy still stared at nothing, but his breathing was steadying.

“What was in the box?” GoldenPaw asked, in Nepali, as carefully and quietly as he could.

The boy looked at him then. Dark eyes in a small face, round with cold and wide with something that was working its way slowly back from shock toward ordinary fear, which was a kind of progress.

He held up his hands about thirty centimetres apart. Box-shaped.

Then he made a gesture — fingers spread, moving outward and downward from one palm — that GoldenPaw recognised, after a fraction of a second, as an imitation of something spreading. Branching. Radiating.

“A map?” GoldenPaw asked.

The boy shook his head. He made the gesture again — slower, more deliberate — and added a sound. A deep, resonant hum in the back of his throat.

GoldenPaw went very still.

* * *

He left the woman — her name, she told him as he was rising to go, was Pemba; she was the village's health worker, its de facto decision-maker in emergencies, and she had been making decisions in emergencies for long enough that the experience sat on her like a garment she'd worn into shape — he left Pemba with the two disarmed operatives zip-tied to the prayer wheel post with the webbing straps from their own equipment harnesses. It was not a permanent solution. It was a twenty-minute solution, which was all he needed it to be.

He retrieved the detonator case from the canyon before moving south. The operative with the pale eyes was still standing where GoldenPaw had left him, watching his three incapacitated colleagues with an expression that suggested he had already composed the incident report in his head and was not looking forward to submitting it. The fourth man — the one who had been hit with the elbow strike — had regained consciousness and was sitting in the snow with his back against the canyon wall, pressing his jaw with exploratory fingers.

GoldenPaw held up the detonator case.

“I'm going to need you not to have another one of these somewhere,” he said.

The pale-eyed man looked at the case, then at GoldenPaw, with the expression of someone running a calculation that is coming out the same way every time he runs it.

“There is no second set,” he said.

“I believe you about forty percent,” GoldenPaw said. “Which is enough to work with.”

He left the canyon with the case under one arm and the two confiscated sidearms ejected and tucked in the exterior pocket of his suit — the clips he dropped in the snow at two-hundred-metre intervals, each in a different direction. A small precaution. A habit.

The southern edge of Thyangoche opened onto a narrow track that crossed a stone bridge over a glacial meltwater stream and then climbed again — southeast, toward the base of the terminal moraine that marked the lower boundary of the Khumbu's ancient ice. In summer this trail saw regular traffic, bound for the base camp approach. In early winter, in the dying light, it was utterly empty and utterly silent except for the wind off the glacier and the sound of his own breathing.

He found the entrance to the shaft because he smelled it before he saw it. The chemical odour — the one that had been wrong since the ridge, sharp and acrid and now resolving, at close range, into something he could finally name. Oxidised minerals. The particular ferrous-and-sulphur combination that results when certain kinds of deep rock are exposed to open air for the first time in millennia. Not a natural smell, here, at this altitude. A smell that had no business existing at the surface.

The shaft entrance was concealed under a steel-framed hatch built into the base of a large boulder, the surrounding ground graded and repacked to look undisturbed from more than ten metres away. The work was months old, at least — the packed earth had settled and the sedge grass at the perimeter had regrown to a natural density. Someone had been very patient, and very careful, and had worked through the worst of the winter when the surrounding terrain was deep in snow and the villagers kept to their lower ground.

The hatch was sealed with an electronic lock. GoldenPaw looked at it for two seconds, noted the model and series — the same class of lock used in military forward-operating installations, which confirmed that whoever was running this operation had significant procurement connections — and then applied the flat of his palm and approximately sixty kilograms of focused leverage to the hinged edge. The lock held. The hinge did not.

He descended.

The shaft had been bored cleanly, a one-metre diameter tube angled at approximately forty degrees into the bedrock. Metal rungs had been drilled into one side — the other was studded with cable conduits and bundled wiring, running down into the dark. Cold. Colder than the surface, which was not what he had expected. At this depth, geothermal gradient should have begun warming the rock. The cold meant either very deep insulation from a significant cold source above — the glacier, plausibly — or something else. Something actively cold. Something that was consuming heat rather than merely lacking it.

The shaft opened at the bottom into a chamber.

GoldenPaw stood in the entrance of it and took a very long, very slow breath.

The chamber was larger than the shaft had suggested — perhaps fifteen metres across, roughly elliptical, with a ceiling that rose at its apex to four or five metres. The walls were naked rock, bored smooth by industrial equipment and lit by a string of cold LED work lights anchored to the ceiling. Two portable power units hummed in the corner, diesel, their exhaust piped upward through a small secondary shaft. Equipment tables. Laptops, open and dark. A large-format printer with a topographic output still curled in its output tray. Foam-padded cases stacked against one wall — open, and empty. Whatever they had contained had been removed recently.

In the centre of the chamber floor, the rock had been removed in a precise square, two metres by two, and what lay beneath was not more rock.

He crossed to the excavation and looked down.

It was crystalline. That was the first word his mind arrived at, and then immediately discarded as insufficient. It was crystalline the way a cathedral window is crystalline — structure at a scale beyond individual description, individual facets combining into something that operated at a different level of meaning entirely. The material was pale — almost white in the LED light, but with an inner refraction that shifted it through amber and blue and a green so pale it was nearly silver as he moved his head. It was cold to look at. Not in temperature — though when he extended one hand and held it above the surface he could feel the active chill of it, the aggressive cold that he had noted while descending — but in the way that certain large, very old things are cold: possessed of a kind of indifferent permanence that makes warmth seem briefly irrelevant.

It extended below the excavation without apparent limit. The square hole was nearly a metre deep and the crystalline formation showed no sign of thinning or changing.

He straightened. His mind was running the fragments together. The mineral surveys filed under false names. The seismic sensors in the cases on the trail. The sensor device on the tripod in the village square — aimed at the ground. The boy's gesture: spreading, branching, radiating. The hum.

He looked around the chamber more carefully. At the far end, he now noticed, there was a door — not a hatch but an actual door, metal-framed, heavy, set into the rock wall as though the intention had always been permanence. It was ajar. Through it came a sound, and the sound was the thing the boy had been humming. Low. Resonant. Sub-audible in the way of large machinery or natural geological processes, felt in the chest rather than heard at the ear.

And, from beneath it, barely distinguishable, a human voice. Then another. Then a sharp syllable that might have been a command or a cry — he couldn't yet tell which.

GoldenPaw set the detonator case down beside the excavation, noted its position relative to the ladder, noted the positions of the power units and the exit, and moved toward the door.

He did not open it immediately. He stood to one side and listened.

Two voices. A third, less frequent — lower, older. Kanchha, he guessed, the man Pemba had described. The first voice was giving instructions in a language GoldenPaw placed after a moment as Mandarin — not standard Mandarin but a technical register, dense with compound nouns that suggested engineering or physics. The second voice responded in English, with an eastern European inflection. The words he could isolate were technical and almost meaningless without context: resonance threshold, sample integrity, the third array is still offline.

Then, clearly, in English: “We don't have time for this. The window is six hours. If we don't confirm the extraction parameters before the uplink window closes, we lose three months.”

A pause. The hum.

Then, in a tone that had acquired a specific, purposeful flatness: “Get the porter to carry the core samples. If he won't cooperate, make him.”

GoldenPaw's hand, which had been resting against the door frame, pressed a little more firmly against the stone.

He thought of the boy in the square, with his hands spread to show the shape of the box, and his throat humming with the sound of something ancient and cold and very large. He thought of Kanchha, who had found something in the high meadow and had kept it for two months and told no one, and who had been taken from his doorway by men in black thermal suits who had been told the evacuation was complete.

He thought of the detonators, with their amber status lights blinking quietly in the case he had set beside the crystal.

He pushed the door open and went through.

* * *

The passage beyond was low-ceilinged and short — ten metres, perhaps twelve — and opened into a second chamber that was almost twice the size of the first. Here the work lights were augmented by two larger floods on adjustable stands, their beams directed downward to illuminate a second excavation that occupied nearly half the chamber floor. The crystalline formation was exposed here across a much larger area, its surface marked at regular intervals by sensor arrays — the same tubular grey devices he had seen in the cases on the trail — pressed flat against it and wired to a central data unit blinking rapidly on a folding table to one side.

There were three people in the chamber.

The first was a man of perhaps fifty, slight, wearing cold-weather gear over a technical sweater, his hair close-cropped and greying at the temples. He stood beside the data unit with the particular posture of an academic under pressure — shoulders raised, head forward, weight on his front foot, the posture of a mind working faster than the situation is comfortable with. He was the Mandarin speaker. He did not look up when GoldenPaw entered.

The second was larger, younger, with the same black thermal suit as the operatives on the trail and the square, standing on the far side of the excavation with his hands at his sides. He looked up immediately. His hand moved toward his holster with professional speed and stopped when he registered that GoldenPaw was already inside the chamber, already inside the distance at which the holster was useful.

The third was Kanchha.

He was seated on the floor against the chamber's eastern wall, his quilted jacket torn at one shoulder, his wrists bound in front of him with the same kind of heavy zip-tie that Pemba's captives were presently wearing. He was in his fifties, GoldenPaw guessed — a broad-faced man with the careful stillness of someone who has decided that stillness is the correct response to a situation until a better option presents itself. He was looking at GoldenPaw with an expression that had just shifted from endurance into something provisional and watchful.

Two more men crouched at the edge of the excavation, adjusting sensor arrays. They were not the Mandarin speaker and were not, it appeared, armed — the focused, narrow-shouldered absorption of technical work was clearly genuine. They looked up. One of them started to rise.

“Stay where you are,” GoldenPaw said, in a tone that was not loud but was extremely clear. “Everyone stay where you are.”

The armed operative had made a decision. GoldenPaw could see it in the shift of his weight, the recalibration from holster to something else — a combat posture, preparing to use the three hundred pounds of mass and training that a weapon had made unnecessary to bother developing.

“Don't,” GoldenPaw said.

The man came anyway.

He was good. GoldenPaw gave him that. He came with economical violence and a minimum of telegraphing, and in the confined space of the chamber his size was an asset in a way it would not have been in the open. He absorbed the first redirect with a roll of his shoulder that cost him less momentum than it should have, got one hand on GoldenPaw's forearm, and used the grip to try to anchor and control.

The problem was the floor.

The exposed crystalline surface beside the excavation was as smooth as polished concrete and significantly colder. When GoldenPaw planted his right foot to leverage out of the grip, his paw found no traction. He adjusted in the instant before the adjustment was due — not by fighting the slide but by using it, letting his weight drop and his centre of gravity shift so that the operative's forward momentum became a problem for the operative rather than a problem for him. The man went over GoldenPaw's lowered shoulder and into the wall above Kanchha's head with a concussive impact that shook loose a small shower of grit from the bore marks in the stone.

He did not get up.

Kanchha looked at GoldenPaw. Then at the fallen man. Then back at GoldenPaw, with the expression of someone who is revising their provisional assessment in a more favourable direction.

The Mandarin speaker had not moved during any of this. He was still looking at his data unit, his narrow shoulders still raised, his fingers still moving across the keyboard with the distant, absorbed quality of someone so deep in a problem that external events have simply ceased to impinge.

“Who are you?” he said, without turning. His English was accented but precise.

“Someone who would like an explanation,” GoldenPaw said. “Starting with what this is.” He gestured toward the crystalline surface.

The man was quiet for a moment. Then he did turn — and his expression, GoldenPaw noted, was not frightened. It was complicated. It held the particular mixture of grief and exhilaration that GoldenPaw had seen before, in the faces of scientists who have discovered something that cannot be undiscovered, who have pulled a thread and watched something enormous shift.

“It is,” the man said, with the care of someone choosing between words that all feel inadequate, “an answer to a question that most of my field had concluded was unanswerable.” He paused. “It is also, depending on what is done with it, either the most important geological discovery of the century or a weapon of genuinely catastrophic potential.”

The hum from below was a continuous, steady presence in GoldenPaw's chest.

He looked down at the crystal. He looked at the sensor arrays, the data unit, the detonator wiring running in two parallel lines from the data table into the floor via a conduit he hadn't noticed when he entered — narrow, recessed, running along the chamber's edge directly to the excavation perimeter.

He looked back at the man.

“The detonators upstairs,” GoldenPaw said. “Tell me they're not keyed to this.”

The man's complicated expression shifted by one degree.

“They were not supposed to be used without completion of the extraction protocol,” he said. “They were a — contingency. A collapse charge. If the site were compromised before the data transfer completed.” He paused again. “The man who holds the primary trigger is not in this chamber.”

GoldenPaw thought of the pale eyes. The low-ready weapon. We are not your problem tonight.

He thought of the blinking amber status lights, sitting quietly in the case he had set down beside the crystal in the first chamber, with the hatch above it open and the pale-eyed man on the surface.

He was already moving back through the door before the thought had fully completed.
