Chapter One

The Held Breath

The pre-dawn darkness over Xizhazi Village was the kind that pressed down on the world like a hand held flat against water, heavy and absolute. A single lamp burned amber in the window of Old Madam Feng's farmhouse guesthouse, its glow reaching no farther than the gravel courtyard where chickens slept in a silent pile beneath a persimmon tree. Beyond the courtyard, the Huairou mountains rose in black masses against a sky pricked with cold stars, and somewhere above those mountains, invisible but ancient, the Jiankou Great Wall coiled along the ridge like the spine of a sleeping dragon.

GoldenPaw landed on the road outside the village without a sound.

That was the trick most people never understood about him — the speed was easy. Speed was a birthright, something written into the long muscles of his haunches and the deep architecture of his spine. It was the stillness that took effort. He stood at the edge of the gravel lane and let the night settle around him, his spotted coat shifting faintly in the mountain breeze, the gold-threaded panels of his suit catching the starlight and giving it back in warm flickers. His amber eyes — huge and precisely made for the dark — read the village in a single unhurried sweep. Fourteen farmhouses. Three guesthouses. One generator humming behind a concrete wall. No movement.

He reached up and touched the comms receiver behind his left ear.

"I'm at the base," he said quietly. "Xizhazi. All clear."

The voice that came back was clipped and dry, belonging to a woman named Dr. Shen Wei who operated out of a monitoring station in Beijing's Huairou District and who had, in GoldenPaw's experience, never once wasted a syllable. "Confirmed. Satellite passed three minutes ago. You have a ninety-minute window before the next pass. The signal we flagged is coming from the central section — somewhere between Beijing Knot and the Eagle Flies Facing Upward approach. We still can't isolate it further."

"Understood." He looked up the mountain. A thin trail threaded from the village through a stand of pale-barked white aspen trees, climbing steeply into the dark. "What am I looking for, exactly?"

A pause — unusually long for Dr. Shen. "We don't know. The signal doesn't match any registered equipment. It isn't radio, it isn't standard electromagnetic. It's something older." Another pause. "Or something that only looks older."

GoldenPaw's tail moved once, a slow, involuntary arc. He had learned over the years to trust that particular movement. It happened when his body registered something his mind hadn't caught up with yet. "I'll check in from the ridge," he said.

He started up the trail at a walk, which was a choice. The path from Xizhazi to the wall took most hikers an hour and a half. GoldenPaw was not most hikers, and he was also not in a hurry — not yet. The mountain had things to say if you gave it the chance to say them. The aspens thinned and gave way to sumac and wild pomegranate scrub, their branches leafless in the late-autumn cold, their roots working silently into the ancient lime mortar of walls he couldn't yet see but could already feel, the way you feel a river before you hear it. The air tasted of cold stone and pine resin and something else — something faintly electric, like the charged atmosphere before a storm, except the sky was cloudless and still.

He catalogued it without making a judgment and kept climbing.

The wall appeared as most things appear in the dark — not all at once but gradually, a suggestion of geometry where the mountain had no business being geometric. A battlement notch against the stars. Then a watchtower, its top broken, one wall collapsed inward, sumac trees growing through the gap with patient, destructive ambition. The Ming Dynasty bricks were dark with centuries of weather, many of them stamped with characters he could read in the starlight — provincial names, family names, the signatures of men who had stacked them into place five hundred years ago and never imagined anything like him standing here reading them.

He put his hand on the wall and felt the cold shoot up through his palm.

The electric taste in the air was stronger here. Much stronger. He turned his head slowly, letting his ears pivot forward, and heard something he hadn't expected: silence. Not the silence of an empty mountain, which had its own texture — the shuffle of a night bird, the distant percussion of water over limestone, the creak of bare branches. This was the other kind of silence. The kind that meant something nearby was holding its breath.

GoldenPaw crouched low and moved east along the wall, using the battlement's shadow.

The walkway of the Jiankou section was nothing like the broad, groomed promenade of Mutianyu to the east. Here, it was fractured stone and gaping holes where bricks had simply given up and fallen away into the valley below — a valley that, in the dark, was an unknowable depth of black. He moved across the broken surface with the careful precision of something that had been designed, somewhere deep in its evolution, to cross difficult terrain at speed without looking down. His claws found purchase where a boot would have slipped. His weight shifted forward over his knuckles when the path narrowed to less than a foot of crumbling edge.

Ahead, the wall curved sharply left and began its first serious ascent.

He stopped.

There was a light — not the amber warmth of a lamp and not the blue-white of a phone screen. This was different. It pulsed from somewhere around the curve of the wall, slow and deep, the color of heated copper, and it threw no shadows in the conventional sense. Instead, it seemed to absorb the existing darkness around it, drawing the night in rather than pushing it back. GoldenPaw watched it pulse twice, three times, each beat timed like a heartbeat at rest, unhurried and patient.

He pressed himself against the inner face of the battlement and edged forward another ten meters.

The source came into view.

It sat in the center of the cracked walkway, directly between two intact sections of crenellation, and it was roughly the size and shape of a large river stone — smooth, dark, and unremarkable except for the copper light that breathed in and out of its surface. Around it, the ancient bricks had been arranged in a pattern that was clearly not accidental — eight of them pulled from the nearby rubble and set in a rough circle, each one placed with the brick stamp facing inward. Someone had done this deliberately. Recently, given the disturbed frost on the stone.

GoldenPaw stayed still for a long moment, studying the object from a distance, his nose working through the cold air. Stone, yes. And something mineral he couldn't name. And beneath that — the faintest trace of a warm-blooded body. Human. Male. Present within the last two hours.

He touched his earpiece. "Dr. Shen. I have eyes on something."

"Describe it."

He did. The silence on her end lasted long enough that he checked the connection twice. When she spoke again, her clipped voice had changed quality in a way that was subtle and therefore alarming. "Don't touch it," she said. "Don't touch it yet. I need to make a call."

"That's not reassuring."

"No," she agreed, with unusual candor. "It isn't."

GoldenPaw held his position, crouched in the shadow of a Ming Dynasty watchtower that was slowly becoming a tree, while the copper light pulsed on in the dark below him and the mountains held their breath and the stars moved their imperceptible inch across the sky. Somewhere far down the valley, a rooster crowed — absurdly early, as if the bird had looked up at this mountain and decided that morning should begin immediately, out of an excess of caution.

He understood the impulse entirely.

The wind shifted, sliding down off the high ridge to the west, and brought with it something new — not a smell this time but a sound. Footsteps. Deliberate, measured, and moving up the wall toward him from the east, from the direction of the Mutianyu connection. Someone who knew this path. Someone who had walked it before.

GoldenPaw didn't move. He waited. In his experience, the most useful thing speed could do was remain entirely potential.

Chapter Two

It Only Calls

The footsteps were unhurried. That was the first thing. Whoever was moving east-to-west along the Jiankou walkway was not fleeing anything and was not searching in the nervous, sweeping way of someone who had lost something. They moved with the steady, economical pace of a person who had made this walk enough times to stop being afraid of it — the broken stones, the unguarded drops, the sections where the wall narrowed to nothing above the void.

GoldenPaw counted the steps. Approximately sixty kilograms. Single person. No additional gear weight beyond what the cadence suggested was a light pack. He pressed himself flatter against the battlement's inner face and waited.

The figure came around the curve of the wall carrying no light at all.

That was the second thing.

He was a man of perhaps sixty years, slight in the way that mountainous country makes people slight — not thin, but compacted, every unnecessary thing burned away by altitude and use. He wore a quilted work jacket the color of old moss, trousers tucked into rubber-soled boots, and a knit cap pulled low over his ears. He moved with his hands slightly out from his sides for balance, feeling the wall's texture with the back of his knuckles the way a blind man reads a familiar room. His eyes, when GoldenPaw caught their angle, were fixed not on the copper-glowing stone in the center of the walkway but on the ground a meter ahead of his next step.

He already knew the stone was there.

He slowed when he reached the edge of the brick circle and stood looking down at it with an expression GoldenPaw couldn't name from this distance — something between grief and a long patience, the face of someone waiting for a bus that has been delayed before and will be delayed again.

GoldenPaw dropped silently from the battlement shadow onto the walkway four meters behind him.

The man didn't startle. He spoke without turning, in Mandarin, his voice carrying the particular flatness of the Huairou mountain dialect. "I wondered if they would send something faster than a helicopter this time."

"Something," GoldenPaw said, in the same language.

The man turned then. He looked at GoldenPaw with the frank, unhurried appraisal of someone accustomed to unusual things appearing on this wall at night, and appeared to arrive at a conclusion that neither alarmed nor reassured him. He glanced at the copper-pulsing stone. Then back. "You haven't touched it."

"I was told not to."

A breath that might have been the beginning of a laugh. "Whoever told you that," the old man said, "knows enough to be cautious and not enough to be useful." He crouched at the edge of the brick circle with the easy flexibility of a lifelong climber and studied the stone's surface the way GoldenPaw had studied the Ming brick stamps — reading something there, though whatever language it was written in was not one GoldenPaw recognized. "It won't hurt you. It doesn't do anything to the person who finds it. It only — " He paused, searching. "It only calls."

The copper light pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient and slow as a resting heart.

"Calls to what?" GoldenPaw said.

The old man looked up at him, and in his eyes was the particular sorrow of a man who had been waiting a very long time to be asked exactly that question and was not at all certain he wanted to answer it now that the moment had finally arrived.

Chapter Three

The Place Below the Place

The old man sat down on the cold stone beside the brick circle as though the question had taken something structural out of him, some internal brace that had been holding him upright for longer than was sustainable. He pulled his knit cap down an inch further over his ears. The copper light moved across his face in slow, warm pulses.

"My name is Chen Baolin," he said. "I have lived in Nanjili — the small village at the foot of this section — for sixty-one years. My father lived there before me. His father before him." He paused. "Three generations of us have been watching this stone come back."

GoldenPaw moved away from the battlement and crouched on the walkway at the edge of the brick circle, close enough now to feel the faint warmth the stone was giving off — and it was warmth, he realized, not just light. A directed, deliberate warmth, like the heat from a lamp aimed at a specific point in the room. He looked at Chen Baolin. "Come back from where?"

The old man gestured vaguely eastward, toward the ridge line, toward the direction that, in the dark, suggested nothing except more wall, more mountain, more cold. "We don't have a word for it. My grandfather called it the place below the place. He was not a poetic man. He meant it literally." He tapped his boot against the stone walkway. "Down. But not in any direction you can point to."

GoldenPaw's earpiece was silent. Dr. Shen was still on her call, or had decided to listen.

"How often does it come back?" he asked.

"Every eleven years. Sometimes twelve. The last time was 2012. Before that, 2001." Chen Baolin looked at the stone with that bus-delayed expression again — the patience of a man who had stopped being surprised by the route's unreliability without ever fully accepting it. "It appears at the Upward Flying Eagle Tower first. Always there. Then it — migrates, I suppose you would say — westward along the wall, one section per night, until it reaches Beijing Knot. And then it stops. And it calls."

"For how long?"

"Three nights. If nothing answers by the third night, it goes back down." He spread his hands in his lap, palms up — a gesture that was not quite a shrug and not quite a surrender. "And we wait eleven years."

"And has anything ever answered?"

The silence that followed this question was the longest yet. A wind moved across the ridge above them, raking the dry sumac branches against the dolomite. Far below the wall's southern face, in the lightless valley, something stirred in the undergrowth — a fox, perhaps, or one of the small deer that came down out of the Heitou Mountain forests in the cold months. GoldenPaw heard it and filed it and did not look away from Chen Baolin's face.

"Once," the old man said. "1957. My grandfather was twenty-two years old." He stopped. The copper light pulsed between them, patient as a tide. "He never told anyone what answered. He told my father only that it had been wrong. That the thing that came had not been what the stone was calling for." His voice stayed flat throughout this, the Huairou mountain dialect ironing out inflection the way the altitude ironed out unnecessary effort, but his hands had closed in his lap. "There were — consequences. For the wall. For the village. It took a long time to correct."

GoldenPaw looked at the stone. The warmth coming off it was steady and, he had to admit, not unpleasant — like the memory of sunlight on a surface that had been absorbing it all day and was only now giving it back. He found himself understanding why someone, five hundred years ago or last Tuesday, might have set bricks in a circle around it. The impulse was less superstitious than it appeared. It was the impulse to mark a border. To say: here is the thing, and here is the space we are giving it, and we are asking you to notice the difference.

"This is the second night?" he said.

Chen Baolin nodded.

"Then we have tonight and one more after."

"Yes."

GoldenPaw's earpiece clicked. Dr. Shen's voice came back, and whatever call she had made had not improved her usual economy of language. If anything, it had compressed it further. "GoldenPaw. The signal type has been preliminarily identified. It matches records from a 1957 incident in the Huairou District that were sealed by the relevant authorities and have only just been partially unlocked for me. I cannot tell you everything yet. What I can tell you is that the signal is a — " A pause, which from Dr. Shen carried the weight of a full paragraph. "A request. The object is broadcasting a specific frequency that has a known — known to certain people — response frequency. Something is supposed to answer it."

"I know," GoldenPaw said.

A beat. "You've spoken to someone."

"Chen Baolin. He's been watching this for sixty years."

Another pause, longer, and he could almost hear her recalibrating. "Ask him," she said carefully, "what the correct response frequency is."

GoldenPaw looked at the old man. Chen Baolin had heard the voice through the earpiece — not the words, but the fact of another person — and was watching GoldenPaw with an expression that had shifted in quality. The patience was still there, but it had acquired a new texture. Something like attention. Something like the specific alertness of a man who has waited eleven years in an outer room and has just heard footsteps approaching the door from the other side.

"The person I work with wants to know what the right response is," GoldenPaw said. "What the stone is actually calling for."

The old man was quiet for a moment. Then he reached inside his quilted jacket and produced a small object — a notebook, battered, its cover reinforced with a strip of electrical tape, its pages soft with handling. He held it without opening it, feeling its edges with his thumbs.

"My grandfather wrote it down," he said. "After 1957. After he understood what he had done wrong. He spent thirty years writing down what it should have been." He held the notebook out across the copper light, and his arm was steady, but GoldenPaw noticed the deliberateness it took to make it so. "He gave this to my father, and my father gave it to me, and I have carried it up this wall every eleven years since 1990, hoping for someone to give it to."

GoldenPaw looked at the notebook. He did not take it immediately. In his experience — and his experience ran long and strange in directions most people's did not — the weight of an object was often inversely proportional to its size. "Why not use it yourself?" he said. "Why not respond yourself?"

Chen Baolin almost smiled. It was the expression of a man who had asked himself the same question many times, arriving always at the same answer, and had come to a working peace with it without achieving anything like contentment. "My grandfather's notes are very clear on this point. The response must come from something that is not entirely of this place. Not entirely human, he wrote, though he meant something more specific than that." His eyes moved across GoldenPaw's spotted coat, the gold-threaded suit, the amber eyes that were reading him with a precision no human face had ever turned on him. "He didn't know what that would mean. He only wrote what it couldn't be."

The copper light pulsed once, very slowly, and for a moment the warmth of it was strong enough that GoldenPaw felt it on the fur of his forearms, a gentle, sourceless pressure, like the first breath of morning through an open window.

He took the notebook.

It was lighter than he expected. Then heavier. He felt the transition between the two as a fact about the object rather than a fact about his hands.

"I need to read this carefully before we consider acting," he said. "And my colleague in Beijing needs to see what she can cross-reference against the 1957 records." He looked at Chen Baolin. "Will you stay?"

"I've stayed every time since 1990," the old man said. "I'm not going to stop now because there's finally someone here worth staying for."

He reached into the front pocket of his work jacket and produced a thermos — battered aluminum, the kind sold in hardware stores in every small town in northern China — and poured two cups of tea into the thermos cap and the smaller cup nested beneath it. He held one out. GoldenPaw accepted it, which required a small adjustment in his grip to accommodate the suit's gauntlets, and the tea was hot and dark and tasted of roasted grain, the kind of taste that was less about flavor than about the reliable fact of warmth.

They sat on either side of the copper-pulsing stone in the middle of the Jiankou Great Wall while the stars continued their slow rotation and the notebook lay open across GoldenPaw's knees, and he began to read.

Chen Baolin's grandfather had written in a cramped, meticulous hand, the characters formed with the careful deliberateness of someone who had learned to write late in life and never stopped being slightly amazed that he could do it. The first twenty pages were observation — dates, times, descriptions of the stone's appearance and behavior across what were apparently four full cycles he had personally witnessed, beginning in 1923 and ending in 1957. GoldenPaw read these quickly, cataloguing. The stone was consistent. The brick circle, he read with interest, was not the old man's innovation — it had been there the first time Chen Baolin's grandfather found it, in 1923, and the bricks had been different then, older, pre-Ming, their stamps worn to near-illegibility. Someone before him had made the circle. Someone had always been making it.

Then the 1957 entry, and the handwriting changed — not the characters themselves but the pressure behind them, as if the pen had been held more tightly. GoldenPaw read slowly.

The thing that came was made of the wall's own want. That is how I understood it afterward. It had read the call wrong — or not wrong, exactly, but it had answered only the part of the call that spoke in frequencies it already knew. The wall is old and it has accumulated wanting the way a river accumulates silt. I gave it something to hold and it held too hard. The three towers on the north face — the ones my family called the Three Watchers — their foundations cracked inward rather than outward. As if something inside them had tried to leave.

GoldenPaw paused on this paragraph. He read it again. Then he touched his earpiece.

"Dr. Shen. The 1957 incident. Were there structural failures reported? Specifically in the northern face watchtowers — three of them."

The response took forty seconds. "Yes. Attributed at the time to frost damage and poor maintenance. Three towers on the north slope, foundations compromised inward. The investigation was closed in six weeks." A pause. "The report that was sealed was the second investigation. The one that took eleven months and was never attributed to frost."

GoldenPaw looked up from the notebook. The sky to the east was still absolute black, but the quality of it had changed — not lighter yet, but less committed to darkness, as if the deep hours of night had begun their slow withdrawal. An hour, perhaps ninety minutes, to first light.

Chen Baolin was watching him with the stillness of someone who had read the same pages many times and was now watching someone else arrive at the same moments for the first time, which was its own kind of company.

"The third night is tomorrow," GoldenPaw said.

"Yes."

"And if the right response doesn't come by then, eleven more years."

"My granddaughter is eight years old," Chen Baolin said. "She has asked me why I walk up the wall alone every autumn." He turned his tea cup in his hands. "I have not answered her yet. I would prefer not to hand this to her."

The copper light pulsed between them. The notebook lay open to a page near its center, where Chen Baolin's grandfather had stopped observing and begun, in the careful hand of a man who had made a serious mistake and was determined that it serve some purpose, to theorize. GoldenPaw looked at the page. The characters were denser here, the margins filled with small diagrams — not architectural, not mathematical in any conventional sense, but something in between. Something that described a kind of listening.

He thought about what the old man had said. Not entirely of this place. Not entirely human.

He looked at the Sky Ladder section of the wall in the near distance — nearly vertical steps climbing a ridge so steep that the name was less metaphor than description — and then at the Upward Flying Eagle Tower, dark and broken against the stars, the tower so named because the mountain beneath it forced even eagles into a climbing posture. He had come up from below tonight, from Xizhazi, the way hikers came, the way Chen Baolin came. But the stone had migrated westward from that tower, one section per night, following the wall's line like a thought following the groove it had worn for itself across long years.

Something was meant to come from the other direction.

He read another page.

And then, from somewhere on the eastern ridge — not from the walkway, not from the trail, but from the open mountainside above the wall's northern face — came a sound that was not footsteps and was not wind and was not any animal GoldenPaw had a file for. It was low and continuous and it had a structure to it, a repeating pattern that his ears involuntarily began to parse before his mind had decided whether to.

Chen Baolin had gone very still.

The copper light, for the first time since GoldenPaw had been watching it, changed its rhythm. The slow, patient heartbeat quickened — not to urgency, but to the tempo of a heart that has been resting and has heard something that wakes it.

GoldenPaw closed the notebook. He set his tea down on the stone walkway with the careful placement of someone setting down something he means to return to. He rose from his crouch in the single fluid movement that was, along with the stillness, the other thing most people never quite understood about him — not the speed, which they always watched for, but the quality of transition. The way there was simply no intermediate stage between at rest and in motion.

"Stay here," he said to Chen Baolin. "And stay inside the circle."

The old man looked up at him. The copper light moved in his eyes. "You're going toward it."

"I want to see it before it sees me," GoldenPaw said. "I've found that's usually the better order."

He went north, over the battlement, into the dark of the mountain's outer face, moving low and fast against the dolomite, the notebook tucked inside his suit against his chest, warm from his own warmth and from the fading warmth of the thing on the walkway below, and the sound from the eastern ridge continued its low, structured pulse, neither approaching nor retreating, patient in the way that only very old things or very certain things are patient — the way the wall itself was patient, the way the mountain beneath it was patient, counting time in a currency too large for anything alive to spend in a single life.

Chapter Four

Two Things at Once

The sound had no direction in the conventional sense — it did not grow louder as he moved toward it, did not fade when he paused or shifted course. It simply existed at the same intensity wherever he placed himself on the mountain's outer face, which told him something he filed without yet interpreting: the source was not a point. It was distributed across the ridge the way weather was distributed, the way the cold was distributed, present in every surface at once.

He moved uphill, keeping the wall's northern face to his right, using the exposed dolomite shelves as cover. The birch trees on this slope were older than the ones below the village, their bark deeply furrowed, their crowns reaching at low angles away from the prevailing wind. He passed through them without disturbing a branch.

The sound resolved as he climbed. It was not, he now understood, a single tone. It was a chord — two frequencies braided together, one low enough that he felt it more in his chest than heard it with his ears, one at the upper register of what most humans could detect. He could hear both. He had learned not to consider this unusual. It was simply the range his body had been given, and his body had long since stopped remarking on it.

What struck him was the interval between the two frequencies. It was not a harmonic relationship. It was the interval of a question and a partial answer — someone completing the first phrase of a sentence and leaving the second unresolved, over and over, with the patience of a thing that did not experience waiting as loss.

He stopped behind a limestone shelf six meters above the wall's crenellation and looked east along the ridge.

There was a figure on the mountain.

It stood at the ridgeline against the sky, and the first thing GoldenPaw registered was that it had no heat signature his eyes could resolve — no warmth differentiated it from the stone beneath it or the air around it. It was not cold, exactly. It was the same temperature as everything else, which was different from being cold and was considerably stranger. It had a shape that suggested height without insisting on it, the way a shadow suggests the object casting it without committing to its dimensions. The light around it was not the copper light of the stone below but something older — not a color he had a name for, less seen than acknowledged, the way you acknowledge the presence of a large animal in dense brush before you see it.

GoldenPaw did not move.

The figure's sound continued its braided, incomplete phrase. Waiting for the second half. The wall below responded — not with the stone's quickened heartbeat pulse alone, but with something in the structure of the wall itself, a vibration he felt through the dolomite beneath his palms that he realized had been there since he touched the Ming bricks at the beginning of the night. He had filed it then as the cold in the stone. It was not the cold in the stone.

He touched his earpiece, very slowly, keeping his body absolutely still. "Dr. Shen," he said, barely above breath. "I'm on the northern face, sixty meters east of the stone's position. There is something on the ridgeline. I need you to know that before I make a decision."

The response came back at a murmur, as if she too had intuited the need for quiet across the hundreds of kilometers between them. "Understood. The unlocked records—" A pause. "GoldenPaw, the 1957 records describe a respondent. Not what came, but what was supposed to come. The grandfather's notes — does the notebook mention a sound?"

He moved his hand to his chest, where the notebook pressed against him beneath the suit.

"Not yet," he said. "I haven't finished reading."

"There is a passage in the sealed report describing the correct respondent as something capable of hearing the stone's call on both frequencies simultaneously. Most people only register one. The respondent has to hold both — not choose between them, hold both — and answer in the interval between." A pause that carried the weight of a conclusion she was not comfortable reaching at speed. "The grandfather wrote the same thing thirty years after the incident. He used different language. He said: it must be the kind of thing that knows it is two things at once and is not troubled by this."

The figure on the ridgeline had not moved. The sound continued its incomplete phrase, neither urgent nor diminishing, patient as sedimentation.

GoldenPaw looked at it across the open slope and felt, with the part of himself that had learned to trust involuntary movement, his tail arc once — slowly — against the cold air.

He understood.

He was not here to observe the thing on the ridge. He was not here to protect the wall from it, or to prevent it from answering the stone's call, or to assess whether it constituted a threat to the village below. He had been performing that calculation since he arrived on this mountain, running the variables through the familiar architecture of intervention, and the calculation was wrong. He had been solving for the wrong problem.

The stone was not calling for the figure on the ridge.

The stone was calling for a respondent. The figure on the ridge was the call's second frequency — not the answer, but the other half of the question, the suspended phrase that had been waiting, in some form or another, since longer than the wall had stood here. It was not making a sound. He had been hearing the stone's own broadcast differently now that the two frequencies were within range of each other, the signal suddenly separating into its components the way light separates through a prism.

He pulled the notebook from inside his suit and opened it against his knee in the dark, finding the page by memory of texture. He did not need to read it again. He had read it enough.

Not entirely of this place. Not entirely human. The kind of thing that knows it is two things at once and is not troubled by this.

He looked down at the wall, where the copper light pulsed in its quickened rhythm, and at the figure on the ridge, and at the distance between the two, and felt the notebook's weight shift in his hands — that same transition, lighter and then heavier, a fact about the object and not about him.

Chapter Five

The Right Kind of Thing

He put the notebook back inside his suit.

Then he stood up from behind the limestone shelf and walked down the slope toward the wall, in the open, without cover, the way you walk toward something when concealment has stopped being the relevant question.

He climbed over the battlement and dropped to the walkway. Chen Baolin was where he had left him, sitting beside the brick circle with his thermos cup held in both hands and his eyes on the middle distance that was GoldenPaw coming back across the broken stone. The copper light was moving faster now — not frantically, but with the tempo of a heart that has been still a long time and is remembering what it was made for.

"You know," the old man said. It was not a question.

"I think so," GoldenPaw said. "Your grandfather wrote that it couldn't be entirely human." He stopped at the edge of the brick circle and looked across it. "He also wrote that it couldn't be entirely of this place. He was describing the same thing two ways."

Chen Baolin was quiet, turning his cup in his hands.

"He meant something that lives in two registers at once," GoldenPaw said. "Something that doesn't have to choose." He looked at the stone, at its quickened pulse, at the warmth it was now giving off strongly enough to be visible as the faintest distortion in the cold air above it — not heat haze exactly, but something like the visual equivalent of a held note. "That's what I've been, for as long as I can remember. The difficulty is I spent a long time treating it as a problem to be solved rather than a function to be used."

His earpiece had been silent for several minutes. Dr. Shen, he understood, had arrived at the same conclusion and had the good judgment to let him arrive at it himself.

He stepped inside the brick circle.

The warmth closed around him completely — not uncomfortable, not overwhelming, but total, the way immersion is total, the way you stop feeling the boundary between yourself and the water once you've stopped standing at the edge of it. The stone's pulse was no longer something he was watching from outside. He could feel it in the same place he felt his own heartbeat, as if the two rhythms were occupying the same space in his chest and negotiating, quietly, about the tempo.

He crouched over the stone and placed both palms flat against it.

The cold of earlier was gone. What came up through his palms now was not warmth and not cold but something that had no temperature at all — a kind of structural attention, a quality of being noticed that he had felt from living things before but never from an object. The two frequencies the stone had been broadcasting separated fully in his chest, the low one settling into his sternum and the high one finding the specific register that his ears had been made to hear, and between them was the interval — the gap, the suspended phrase, the question waiting for its second half.

He had no training for this. No protocol. Dr. Shen had no frequency to transmit to him, no procedure from the sealed 1957 report. Chen Baolin's grandfather had spent thirty years writing down what it should have been, and what he had concluded, in the careful hand of a man writing late in life in the margins of his own mistake, was simply: be both, and do not resolve them.

GoldenPaw breathed out.

He let the low frequency stay low. He let the high frequency stay high. He did not try to find the middle of them, did not try to average them into something more manageable or more legible or more like one thing than two. He held them the way he held his own nature — the animal speed and the considered stillness, the birthright and the choice — without making either one wrong for existing alongside the other.

The interval between the frequencies filled.

Not with sound. Not with light. With something that had no sensory correlate he could name, only a quality of completion, the way a lock feels different when the key has fully turned — not dramatic, not loud, simply done, a fact that had been suspended becoming a fact that was settled.

The copper light went out.

Not extinguished. Satisfied — there was a difference, and he felt it clearly. The stone beneath his palms was still warm, but the warmth was now the ordinary warmth of a thing that had been holding heat and was gently releasing it, the way sun-warmed stone releases heat into the evening air, slowly, without urgency, spending what it has been given.

He lifted his hands and stood.

The silence that came back onto the mountain was the other kind — the kind with texture, with the shuffle of a night bird somewhere below the wall's southern face, with the distant percussion of water over limestone, with the creak of a bare birch branch answering the wind off the high ridge. He had not noticed how completely the charged, held-breath silence had displaced it until it returned, and its return was like the return of a smell you hadn't realized was missing.

He looked east along the ridgeline.

The figure was gone. Not fled — there was no sense of retreat, no disturbance in the air suggesting departure. It had simply ceased to be present in the way that a question ceases to be present once it has been answered, not destroyed but dissolved into the fact of its own resolution.

Chen Baolin had risen to his feet. He stood just outside the brick circle, his thermos held at his side, looking at the stone. In the new dark — the stone was truly dark now, ordinary stone, dolomite-gray and unremarkable — his face held an expression that GoldenPaw did not have a name for and suspected had not been on anyone's face often enough to require one. It was the face of a man setting down a weight he had been carrying long enough that he was no longer certain he knew how to stand without it, and was discovering that his spine remembered on its own.

"It's done?" the old man said.

"I think so."

Chen Baolin looked down at the circle of Ming bricks, stamped with their five-hundred-year-old provincial names, their family signatures, and then at the sky, which was changing now — the east had stopped being committed to darkness, the stars in that quarter retreating slightly, the first pale suggestion of a horizon assembling itself behind the Heitou Mountain ridge.

"I should call my daughter," he said, after a while. "She worries when I walk the wall alone before dawn." He paused. "I have been calling her afterward, every eleven years, to tell her nothing happened. This time I would like to call her with something different to say."

GoldenPaw bent down and picked up his tea cup from where he had set it on the walkway. It had gone cold. He drank it anyway, because the grain-roast taste of it was still the most reliably warm thing on this mountain, and because he had said he meant to return to it.

"What will you tell her?" he asked.

Chen Baolin considered this for a long moment, with the thoroughness of a man for whom language was a precise tool used sparingly. He looked at the dark stone, the ordinary stone, sitting at the center of its circle of ancient bricks in the very early morning light. He looked at GoldenPaw.

"That someone came," he said finally, "who was the right kind of thing."

He bent slowly and picked up one of the eight bricks from the circle's edge — not all of them, not the stone, but one brick, held in both hands, turned over to show its stamped face. He looked at it for a moment and then tucked it under his arm with the careful deliberateness of a man taking a small, true thing home.

The sky to the east was unambiguously lighter now. The Jiankou wall ran away in both directions along its impossible ridge, its broken towers and fractured walkway and patient, destructive sumac trees growing through every gap, ancient and indifferent to the night's events in the way that very old structures are indifferent to individual events — not because they do not register them, but because they register everything, and have learned to carry it all without emphasis.

GoldenPaw touched his earpiece one last time.

"Dr. Shen. The signal has resolved."

Her voice came back, and it was the same voice it always was, clipped and economical, every syllable earning its place. But at its edge, so faint it might have been the mountain air playing through the receiver, was a quality he had not heard from her before. "Confirmed," she said. "Signal is gone. We're not reading anything from that section." A pause. "Get some rest before you come back."

He looked at the sky, where the stars were retreating from the east with the dignified unhaste of things that knew they would be back. He looked at the wall running west toward Beijing Knot, and east toward the Upward Flying Eagle Tower, its broken crown just visible against the brightening sky — the tower so named because the mountain forced even eagles into a climbing posture.

He had come up from below, the way hikers came.

He had answered from within.

He thought that Chen Baolin's grandfather, who had been not a poetic man and had meant everything literally, would have found this an acceptable outcome. He thought the grandfather would have written it down in his careful late-learned hand, in the margins, in the precise language of a man who had made a serious mistake and was determined that it serve some purpose, and what he would have written was simply: the place below the place is satisfied. It has what it called for. It will not call again in my lifetime, nor in my children's.

And then he would have closed the notebook, and had his tea, and walked back down the mountain in the early light to his village, the way Chen Baolin was walking now — slowly, with one Ming brick under his arm and one cup of cold tea behind him on the ancient stone, and something upright in his back that had not been there before.

GoldenPaw watched him go until the trail turned and the aspen trees took him. Then he turned east, into the rising light, and ran.
